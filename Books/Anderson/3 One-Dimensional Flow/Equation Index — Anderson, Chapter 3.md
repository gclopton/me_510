# Building Blocks

## 1. Speed of sound — general expression (Eq. 3.17)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the general expression for the speed of sound in any gas.
> (b) Why must the derivative be taken at constant entropy?
> (c) What does this equation tell you about the relationship between compressibility and the speed of sound?
> (d) What does this expression predict in the limit of an incompressible fluid?

> [!equation] Speed of sound — general expression (Eq. 3.17)
>
> $$ a^{2}=\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho}\right)_{s} $$

The speed of sound tells you how quickly news about a pressure change travels through a gas. It is fundamentally a measure of the gas's "stiffness": a gas that strongly resists compression (pressure rises steeply when you squeeze it) transmits disturbances quickly, while a soft, easily compressed gas transmits them slowly. The isentropic constraint ($s = \text{const}$) reflects the physics of the sound wave itself — the disturbance is so gentle that friction and heat conduction within it are negligible, making the compression–expansion cycle reversible and adiabatic. In the limit of an incompressible fluid, $a \to \infty$: every part of the fluid "knows" about a pressure change instantaneously, which is precisely what "incompressible" means physically.

|$\rho$|kg/m³|kg·m⁻³|**Density**|The local mass per unit volume of the gas.|
|$p$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Pressure**|The local thermodynamic pressure.|
|$\left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial \rho}\right)_{s}$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Isentropic Pressure–Density Sensitivity**|How much the pressure changes per unit change in density at constant entropy.|
|$a$|m/s|m·s⁻¹|**Speed of Sound**|The propagation speed of infinitesimally weak pressure waves through the gas.|

## 2. Speed of sound for a perfect gas (Eq. 3.20)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the speed-of-sound formula for a calorically perfect gas.
> (b) Why does the speed of sound depend only on temperature for a perfect gas — not on pressure or density independently?
> (c) Why does a monatomic gas have a higher speed of sound than a diatomic gas at the same temperature?
> (d) By what factor does the speed of sound increase if the absolute temperature is doubled?

> [!equation] Speed of sound for a perfect gas (Eq. 3.20)
>
> $$ a=\sqrt{\gamma R T} $$

For a perfect gas, the speed of sound depends on temperature alone — not on pressure or density independently. This makes intuitive sense from a molecular perspective: temperature is a direct measure of how fast the molecules are moving, and sound propagates through molecular collisions, so faster molecules mean faster signal transmission. The factor $\gamma$ captures the gas's internal structure: a monatomic gas ($\gamma = 5/3$) has a higher speed of sound than a diatomic gas ($\gamma = 7/5$) at the same temperature because monatomic molecules store all their thermal energy in translational motion (which propagates sound), whereas diatomic molecules divert some energy into rotations that do not contribute to pressure. Doubling the absolute temperature increases the speed of sound by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$.

|$T$|K|K|**Absolute Temperature**|Must be in Kelvin or Rankine.|

## 3. Mach number

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the definition of the Mach number.
> (b) What flow regimes does it define?
> (c) Show that $M^2$ is proportional to the ratio of kinetic energy to internal energy for a calorically perfect gas.

> [!equation] Mach number
>
> $$ M = \frac{V}{a} $$

The Mach number is the ratio of the local flow speed to the local speed of sound. It is the single most important dimensionless parameter in compressible flow because it determines whether pressure disturbances can propagate upstream ($M < 1$, subsonic) or are swept downstream ($M > 1$, supersonic). For a calorically perfect gas, $\frac{V^2/2}{e} = \frac{\gamma(\gamma-1)}{2}M^2$, so $M^2$ is directly proportional to the ratio of directed kinetic energy to random thermal energy — it measures how much of the fluid's energy budget is in bulk motion versus molecular motion.

## 4. Total (stagnation) temperature definition (Eq. 3.27)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the equation that defines total temperature.
> (b) What does $T_o$ physically represent about the energy content of a fluid element?
> (c) Why does $T_o$ survive unchanged through shock waves and friction, but not through heat addition?
> (d) Can a fast, cool stream and a slow, warm stream have the same $T_o$? Explain physically.

> [!equation] Total (stagnation) temperature definition (Eq. 3.27)
>
> $$ c_{p} T+\frac{u^{2}}{2}=c_{p} T_{o} $$

The total temperature $T_o$ is the temperature a moving gas would reach if you brought it to rest without adding or removing heat. It represents the full energy content of the fluid — both the thermal energy locked in molecular motion and the kinetic energy of the bulk flow — expressed as a single temperature. A fast, cool stream and a slow, warm stream can have the same $T_o$ if their total energy budgets are equal. In any flow where no heat crosses the boundaries — including flows with friction and even flows through shock waves — $T_o$ remains unchanged. It is the most robust conserved quantity in adiabatic gas dynamics, requiring only the absence of heat transfer (not reversibility) to be preserved.

## 5. Total-to-static temperature ratio (Eq. 3.28)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the total-to-static temperature ratio as a function of Mach number.
> (b) What does this equation tell you about how much the temperature increases when a flow is stagnated?
> (c) For $M = 0$, what is the ratio? For $M \to \infty$?

> [!equation] Total-to-static temperature ratio (Eq. 3.28)
>
> $$ \frac{T_{o}}{T}=1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2} $$

This ratio quantifies how much hotter a gas becomes when you imagine bringing it to rest adiabatically. It expresses the fundamental energy trade-off in compressible flow: the faster a gas moves, the colder it is relative to its stagnation state, because a larger share of its total energy budget is invested in bulk motion rather than molecular thermal energy. At $M = 0$ the ratio is unity — a stationary gas is already at its total temperature. At $M = 2$ (for air), $T_o/T = 1.8$: nearly half the total energy is kinetic. This ratio is the single most-used relation in compressible flow because it connects any local measurement of temperature and Mach number to the total temperature, which is conserved in adiabatic flows.

|$T_o$|K|K|**Total Temperature**|The temperature after adiabatic deceleration to rest.|
|$\frac{\gamma-1}{2}M^2$|—|—|**Compressibility Correction**|The fractional temperature rise due to conversion of kinetic energy to thermal energy.|

## 6. Total-to-static pressure ratio (Eq. 3.30)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the isentropic total-to-static pressure ratio.
> (b) What does total pressure physically represent about the flow's potential to do useful work?
> (c) Why is total pressure "fragile" — destroyed by shocks and friction — while total temperature is "robust"?
> (d) What does a loss of total pressure tell you about the efficiency of a flow process?

> [!equation] Total-to-static pressure ratio (Eq. 3.30)
>
> $$ \frac{p_{o}}{p}=\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2}\right)^{\gamma /(\gamma-1)} $$

The total pressure $p_o$ is the pressure a gas would reach if brought to rest without any losses — a perfect, reversible deceleration. It embodies the maximum work-producing potential of the flow. Unlike total temperature (which survives shocks and friction unchanged), total pressure is fragile: any irreversibility — a shock wave, friction, turbulent mixing — destroys total pressure permanently. This makes $p_o$ the definitive measure of flow quality and aerodynamic efficiency. A high total pressure means the flow retains its ability to accelerate, produce thrust, or do useful work; a depleted total pressure means that energy has been irreversibly degraded into heat. The exponent $\gamma/(\gamma-1)$ reflects how strongly the pressure compounds with the temperature rise during isentropic stagnation.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$p$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Static Pressure**|The actual thermodynamic pressure of the moving fluid.|
|$p_o$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Total (Stagnation) Pressure**|The pressure achieved by isentropically decelerating the flow to rest.|

## 7. Total-to-static density ratio (Eq. 3.31)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the isentropic total-to-static density ratio.
> (b) How does the exponent here differ from the pressure-ratio exponent, and why?

> [!equation] Total-to-static density ratio (Eq. 3.31)
>
> $$ \frac{\rho_{o}}{\rho}=\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2}\right)^{1 /(\gamma-1)} $$

The total density $\rho_o$ is the density that would exist if the gas were isentropically brought to rest. When a moving gas is stagnated, it is compressed into a smaller volume (density rises) and simultaneously heated (temperature rises). Because pressure depends on both density and temperature ($p = \rho R T$), the pressure increases more dramatically than the density during this process. The smaller exponent $1/(\gamma-1)$ compared to the pressure ratio's $\gamma/(\gamma-1)$ reflects this: density grows more gently than pressure during isentropic compression because part of the pressure rise comes from the temperature increase rather than from the compression alone.

|$\rho$|kg/m³|kg·m⁻³|**Static Density**|The actual density of the moving fluid.|
|$\rho_o$|kg/m³|kg·m⁻³|**Total (Stagnation) Density**|The density achieved by isentropically decelerating the flow to rest.|

## 8. Characteristic speed of sound ($a^*$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the definition of the characteristic speed of sound.
> (b) What reference state does $a^*$ correspond to?
> (c) Under what conditions is $a^*$ constant throughout a flowfield?

> [!equation] Characteristic speed of sound ($a^*$)
>
> $$ a^{*}=\sqrt{\gamma R T^{*}} $$

The characteristic speed of sound $a^*$ is a universal reference velocity for any adiabatic flow. It answers the question: "If this particular fluid element were adiabatically sped up or slowed down until it reached exactly Mach 1, what would its speed of sound be?" Because this thought experiment conserves total energy, $a^*$ encodes the total enthalpy of the flow in a single velocity scale. In any adiabatic flowfield, every streamline shares the same $a^*$ — it is a global constant that serves as a natural yardstick against which to measure local velocities. The characteristic Mach number $M^* = u/a^*$ is bounded (unlike $M = u/a$, which can go to infinity), making $a^*$ especially convenient for analyzing shock waves and other phenomena where $M$ spans a wide range.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$T^*$|K|K|**Critical Temperature**|The temperature that would exist if the flow were adiabatically adjusted to Mach 1.|
|$a^*$|m/s|m·s⁻¹|**Characteristic Speed of Sound**|The speed of sound at the hypothetical sonic condition; constant in adiabatic flows.|

## 9. Energy equation in terms of $a^*$ (Eq. 3.26)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the energy equation in the form that involves $a^*$.
> (b) What does the term $a^2/(\gamma-1)$ represent physically, and why is $a$ a proxy for temperature?
> (c) As a gas accelerates, what happens to its local speed of sound, and why?
> (d) Why is $a^*$ the same on both sides of a normal shock, and what does that enable?

> [!equation] Energy equation in terms of $a^*$ (Eq. 3.26)
>
> $$ \frac{a^{2}}{\gamma-1}+\frac{u^{2}}{2}=\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)} a^{* 2} $$

This equation expresses the total energy of the flow as a competition between thermal energy (represented by $a^2$, since sound speed is a proxy for temperature) and kinetic energy ($u^2/2$). The right-hand side is a constant — the same constant at every point in an adiabatic flow — anchored by $a^{*2}$, the characteristic speed of sound. Physically, it says: as a fluid element speeds up, it must cool down (decreasing $a$), and as it slows, it must heat up (increasing $a$), always in exact proportion to keep the total energy budget balanced. The power of this form is that both sides of a normal shock share the same $a^*$ (since the shock is adiabatic), so the energy constraint links upstream and downstream conditions through a single, universal reference velocity.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$a^2/(\gamma-1)$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Thermal Energy Term**|Proportional to $c_p T$; represents the thermal contribution to total enthalpy.|
|$u^2/2$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Kinetic Energy Term**|The macroscopic kinetic energy per unit mass.|
|$\frac{\gamma+1}{2(\gamma-1)}a^{*2}$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Constant Total Enthalpy**|A constant of the adiabatic flow, expressed in terms of the critical speed of sound.|

## 10. Critical-to-total temperature ratio ($T^*/T_o$) (Eq. 3.34)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the ratio $T^*/T_o$.
> (b) For air ($\gamma = 1.4$), what is the numerical value?
> (c) What are the corresponding $p^*/p_o$ and $\rho^*/\rho_o$ ratios?

> [!equation] Critical-to-total temperature ratio (Eq. 3.34)
>
> $$ \frac{T^{*}}{T_{o}}=\frac{2}{\gamma+1} $$

This ratio says that sonic conditions always occur at a fixed energy partition: for air, the temperature at $M = 1$ is always 83.3% of the total temperature, regardless of where you are in the flow or how you got there. The remaining 16.7% of the total enthalpy is kinetic energy. These fixed fractions ($T^*/T_o = 0.833$, $p^*/p_o = 0.528$, $\rho^*/\rho_o = 0.634$ for air) define the "sonic benchmark" — the universal thermodynamic state at Mach 1 for a given total condition. They appear at the throat of every choked nozzle and serve as the reference point for both Rayleigh-line and Fanno-line flow tables. The ratio $2/(\gamma+1)$ depends only on the molecular structure of the gas (through $\gamma$), not on any flow details.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$T^*$|K|K|**Critical Temperature**|The temperature at the sonic condition ($M = 1$).|
|$T_o$|K|K|**Total Temperature**|The stagnation temperature.|
|$2/(\gamma+1)$|—|—|**Critical Temperature Fraction**|The universal ratio of critical to total temperature for a given $\gamma$; equals 0.833 for $\gamma = 1.4$.|

## 11. Relation between $M$ and $M^*$ (Eq. 3.37)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the relation connecting the actual Mach number $M$ to the characteristic Mach number $M^*$.
> (b) What are the limits of $M^*$ as $M \to 0$, $M = 1$, and $M \to \infty$?
> (c) Why is $M^*$ useful for shock wave analysis?

> [!equation] Relation between $M$ and $M^*$ (Eq. 3.37)
>
> $$ M^{2}=\frac{2}{\left[(\gamma+1) / M^{* 2}\right]-(\gamma-1)} $$

$M^*$ re-measures the flow speed against a fixed ruler ($a^*$) instead of the local, ever-changing ruler ($a$). Because $a^*$ is constant in adiabatic flow while $a$ varies with temperature, $M^*$ compresses the infinite range of $M$ into a bounded interval: $M^* = 0$ when the flow is at rest, $M^* = 1$ at sonic conditions, and $M^* \to \sqrt{(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)} \approx 2.449$ (for air) as $M \to \infty$. This boundedness reflects the physical reality that even at infinite Mach number, the flow velocity is finite (it cannot exceed $\sqrt{2 c_p T_o}$, the speed obtained by converting all thermal energy to kinetic). The Prandtl relation $M_1^* M_2^* = 1$ across a normal shock has a beautiful physical meaning: if the upstream flow exceeds the sonic reference ($M_1^* > 1$), the downstream flow must fall below it ($M_2^* < 1$) by exactly the reciprocal amount.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$M = u/a$|—|—|**Mach Number**|The actual Mach number, ratio of flow speed to local speed of sound.|
|$M^* = u/a^*$|—|—|**Characteristic Mach Number**|The ratio of flow speed to the critical speed of sound; bounded and approaches a finite limit as $M \to \infty$.|

## 12. Mass flux ($\rho u$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the expression for mass flux in one-dimensional flow.
> (b) What conservation law keeps this quantity constant between two stations?
> (c) Why is mass flux (rather than velocity alone) the fundamental transport quantity?

> [!equation] Mass flux
>
> $$ \rho u $$

The mass flux is the mass per unit time per unit area flowing through a cross-section. It is the product of density and velocity, and is the fundamental quantity that appears in every flux integral. In steady one-dimensional constant-area flow, the continuity equation $\rho_1 u_1 = \rho_2 u_2$ requires the mass flux to be the same at every station. Velocity alone is not conserved — as the gas compresses or expands, density changes compensate for velocity changes to keep the mass flow rate constant.

|$\rho u$|kg/(m²·s)|kg·m⁻²·s⁻¹|**Mass Flux**|The mass flow rate per unit area; constant across stations in steady 1-D constant-area flow.|

## 13. Impulse function ($p + \rho u^2$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the impulse function and explain what it represents.
> (b) Why is it this particular combination — not $p$ or $\rho u^2$ individually — that is conserved?
> (c) Under what conditions is the impulse function constant between two stations?

> [!equation] Impulse function
>
> $$ p + \rho u^{2} $$

The impulse function is the combination of static pressure and momentum flux per unit area that appears in the one-dimensional momentum equation. It represents the total "push" the flow exerts at a given cross-section — partly from the random molecular motion (pressure) and partly from the directed bulk motion ($\rho u^2$). In a steady, inviscid, one-dimensional flow with no body forces, the momentum equation requires $p_1 + \rho_1 u_1^2 = p_2 + \rho_2 u_2^2$: the impulse function is conserved between stations. This is because there are no external forces acting in the flow direction; the only forces are the pressure forces at the two faces of the control volume.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$p$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Static Pressure**|The pressure contribution from random molecular motion.|
|$\rho u^2$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Momentum Flux per Unit Area**|The pressure-equivalent contribution from directed bulk motion.|
|$p + \rho u^2$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Impulse Function**|The total mechanical "push" per unit area at a cross-section; conserved in steady inviscid 1-D flow without body forces.|

## 14. Total enthalpy ($h_o = h + u^2/2$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the definition of total enthalpy.
> (b) Under what conditions is total enthalpy constant along a streamline?
> (c) A shock wave produces enormous irreversible losses — why doesn't it change the total enthalpy?
> (d) What is the difference between conserving total energy (amount) and conserving total pressure (quality)?

> [!equation] Total enthalpy
>
> $$ h_{o} = h + \frac{u^{2}}{2} $$

Total enthalpy is the complete energy passport of a fluid element in motion. It accounts for everything the element carries: the thermal energy of its molecules, the mechanical energy stored as pressure (the flow work $p/\rho$ needed to push the fluid through its surroundings), and the kinetic energy of its bulk motion. When a gas accelerates through a nozzle, it trades thermal content for speed; when it decelerates in a diffuser, it trades speed for warmth and pressure. Through all of these exchanges, $h_o$ remains unchanged as long as no heat is added or removed. This conservation holds even across shock waves, where enormous irreversible losses occur — those losses degrade the *quality* of the energy (destroying total pressure and creating entropy), but they cannot change the *amount* of total energy.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$h$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Static Enthalpy**|The enthalpy per unit mass at actual flow conditions.|
|$u^2/2$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Kinetic Energy per Unit Mass**|The macroscopic motion energy.|
|$h_o$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Total (Stagnation) Enthalpy**|The total energy per unit mass; constant in steady adiabatic flow.|

## 15. Specific enthalpy ($h = c_p T$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the expression for specific enthalpy of a calorically perfect gas.
> (b) What does $c_p$ represent physically as an "exchange rate" between temperature and energy?
> (c) A gas with high $c_p$ versus low $c_p$ — which experiences larger temperature swings for a given velocity change, and why?
> (d) At approximately what temperature does this linear relationship break down for air, and what physical mechanism causes the breakdown?

> [!equation] Specific enthalpy (calorically perfect gas)
>
> $$ h = c_{p} T $$

For a calorically perfect gas, enthalpy is directly proportional to temperature: knowing the temperature is equivalent to knowing the enthalpy, and vice versa. This simple proportionality means that the energy equation — which is fundamentally a statement about enthalpy — can be rewritten as a statement about temperature, making the entire system of equations solvable in closed form. Physically, $c_p$ represents the thermal capacity of the gas per unit mass per degree: a higher $c_p$ means the gas stores more energy per degree of temperature, so for a given total enthalpy budget, the temperature changes are smaller. For air below about 1000 K, $c_p$ is effectively constant because only translational and rotational molecular energy modes are active. Above that temperature, molecular vibrations begin to absorb energy, $c_p$ rises, and the linear relationship between $h$ and $T$ breaks down.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$c_p$|J/(kg·K)|m²·s⁻²·K⁻¹|**Specific Heat at Constant Pressure**|The energy required to raise the temperature of a unit mass by one degree at constant pressure; constant for a calorically perfect gas.|
|$T$|K|K|**Static Temperature**|The actual thermodynamic temperature.|
|$h = c_p T$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Specific Enthalpy**|The enthalpy per unit mass; linear in temperature for a calorically perfect gas.|

## 16. Ideal gas equation of state ($p = \rho R T$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the ideal gas equation of state.
> (b) Physically, what does this equation say about the molecular origin of pressure?
> (c) Why does a lighter gas (smaller molecular weight $M$) exert more pressure per unit density at a given temperature?
> (d) Under what physical conditions does this equation break down?

> [!equation] Ideal gas equation of state
>
> $$ p = \rho R T $$

The ideal gas law is the bridge between the mechanical world ($p$, $\rho$) and the thermal world ($T$) of a gas. It says that pressure is the product of density, the gas constant, and temperature — meaning pressure arises from molecules bouncing off surfaces, with the intensity determined by how many molecules are present (density) and how fast they move (temperature). The specific gas constant $R = \mathcal{R}/M$ encodes the molecular identity of the gas: lighter molecules (smaller $M$) move faster at a given temperature and exert more pressure per unit density. This equation is valid whenever the molecules spend most of their time flying freely rather than interacting — an excellent approximation for gases at everyday and aerospace conditions, breaking down only near condensation, at extreme pressures, or at cryogenic temperatures.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$p$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Pressure**|The thermodynamic pressure.|
|$\rho$|kg/m³|kg·m⁻³|**Density**|The mass per unit volume.|
|$R$|J/(kg·K)|m²·s⁻²·K⁻¹|**Specific Gas Constant**|$\mathcal{R}/M$; 287 J/(kg·K) for air.|
|$T$|K|K|**Absolute Temperature**|Must be in Kelvin or Rankine.|

## 17. Isentropic relation ($p v^\gamma = \text{const}$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the isentropic relation for a calorically perfect gas.
> (b) What two physical conditions must hold for this relation to be valid?
> (c) What does the exponent $\gamma$ physically control about how the gas responds to isentropic compression?
> (d) Why does a monatomic gas ($\gamma = 5/3$) resist compression more vigorously than a diatomic gas ($\gamma = 7/5$)?

> [!equation] Isentropic relation
>
> $$ p v^{\gamma} = \text{const} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \frac{p_2}{p_1} = \left(\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1}\right)^{\gamma} = \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)} $$

This relation governs how pressure, density, and temperature change together when a gas is compressed or expanded without friction or heat transfer. The exponent $\gamma$ is the key: it determines how "stiff" the gas is during isentropic processes. A higher $\gamma$ means pressure rises more steeply for a given compression — the gas resists being squeezed more vigorously. Physically, $\gamma = c_p/c_v$ reflects how the gas partitions energy among its molecular degrees of freedom; a gas with fewer internal modes (monatomic, $\gamma = 5/3$) channels more energy into pressure during compression than one with many modes (diatomic, $\gamma = 7/5$). This relation is the backbone of compressible flow thermodynamics: it connects the total-to-static property ratios, determines the speed of sound, and defines the isentropic reference process against which all real (irreversible) processes are compared.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$p v^\gamma$|Pa·(m³/kg)$^\gamma$|—|**Isentropic Invariant**|A quantity that remains constant along an isentropic process for a calorically perfect gas.|
|$\gamma$|—|—|**Ratio of Specific Heats**|$c_p/c_v$; controls the steepness of isentropic compression.|

## 18. Entropy change (general thermodynamic) (Eq. 1.36)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the entropy change between two states for a calorically perfect gas.
> (b) Why does entropy depend on both temperature ratio and pressure ratio?
> (c) What does $s_2 - s_1 > 0$ physically signify?

> [!equation] Entropy change
>
> $$ s_{2}-s_{1}=c_{p} \ln \frac{T_{2}}{T_{1}}-R \ln \frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}} $$

Entropy measures the irreversible degradation of a gas's energy quality. This expression reveals two competing influences: heating the gas (raising $T$) increases entropy (the energy becomes more disordered), while compressing it (raising $p$) decreases entropy (the molecules are forced into a more ordered, compact state). When $s_2 > s_1$, the process has irreversibly degraded the gas's ability to do useful work — energy has been scattered into forms that cannot be fully recovered. In a normal shock, both temperature and pressure rise, but the temperature rise "wins" and entropy increases. This entropy increase is the thermodynamic fingerprint of irreversibility — it is why only compression shocks ($M_1 > 1$) are physically realizable, and why expansion shocks ($M_1 < 1$, which would decrease entropy) are forbidden by the second law.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$s$|J/(kg·K)|m²·s⁻²·K⁻¹|**Specific Entropy**|The entropy per unit mass.|
|$c_p \ln(T_2/T_1)$|J/(kg·K)|m²·s⁻²·K⁻¹|**Temperature Contribution**|The entropy change due to temperature variation.|
|$R \ln(p_2/p_1)$|J/(kg·K)|m²·s⁻²·K⁻¹|**Pressure Contribution**|The entropy change due to pressure variation (with opposite sign).|

## 19. Kinetic energy per unit mass ($u^2/2$)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the kinetic energy per unit mass.
> (b) In the energy equation, what role does this term play relative to enthalpy?
> (c) When is this term negligible compared to the enthalpy?

> [!equation] Kinetic energy per unit mass
>
> $$ \frac{u^2}{2} $$

The kinetic energy per unit mass measures how much of the fluid's total energy is invested in bulk motion rather than molecular thermal activity. It is the "mechanical" half of the total enthalpy budget $h_o = h + u^2/2$. At low Mach numbers ($M \ll 1$), kinetic energy is a tiny fraction of the total — the gas is essentially "all heat and no speed" — and temperature hardly changes along the flow. This is why compressibility effects are negligible in low-speed aerodynamics. As $M$ grows, kinetic energy becomes an increasingly dominant share of the energy budget: at $M = 2$ for air, roughly 44% of the total enthalpy is kinetic. This growing dominance is what makes the energy equation essential in high-speed flow — you can no longer ignore the temperature changes that accompany velocity changes.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$u$|m/s|m·s⁻¹|**Flow Velocity**|The one-dimensional velocity.|
|$u^2/2$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Specific Kinetic Energy**|The kinetic energy per unit mass of the bulk fluid motion.|

## 20. Friction coefficient definition

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the definition of the local friction coefficient.
> (b) What does it represent physically?
> (c) What typical value is used for turbulent flow in a smooth duct?

> [!equation] Friction coefficient
>
> $$ \tau_{w} = \frac{1}{2} \rho u^{2} f $$

The friction coefficient $f$ (also called the Fanning friction factor) relates the wall shear stress $\tau_w$ to the dynamic pressure $\frac{1}{2}\rho u^2$ of the flow. It is a dimensionless measure of how effectively friction retards the flow — a higher $f$ means more momentum is being extracted from the bulk flow by wall shear per unit of dynamic pressure. The value of $f$ depends on the Reynolds number, surface roughness, and whether the flow is laminar or turbulent. For most practical applications with turbulent flow and moderate roughness, $f \approx 0.005$ is a reasonable approximation.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$\tau_w$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Wall Shear Stress**|The frictional force per unit area exerted by the fluid on the duct wall.|
|$\frac{1}{2}\rho u^2$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Dynamic Pressure**|The kinetic energy per unit volume of the flow; the reference scale for the shear stress.|
|$f$|—|—|**Friction Coefficient**|The dimensionless ratio of wall shear to dynamic pressure; $\approx 0.005$ for turbulent flow with $Re > 10^5$.|

# Assumptions & Simplifications

## 21. Calorically Perfect Gas

> [!question]
>
> (a) What does the calorically perfect gas assumption state about $c_p$ and $c_v$?
> (b) What temperature range is this assumption valid for air?
> (c) What breaks down at high temperatures, and what must be done instead?

> [!assumption] Calorically Perfect Gas
>
> The specific heats $c_p$ and $c_v$ (and hence $\gamma = c_p/c_v$) are constants, independent of temperature.

**Definition.** A calorically perfect gas has constant specific heats at all temperatures. This means $h = c_p T$, $e = c_v T$, and the isentropic relation $p v^\gamma = \text{const}$ all hold with constant coefficients. All the closed-form analytical results in Chapter 3 — the normal shock relations, the Rayleigh-line relations, the Fanno-line relations — depend on this assumption.

**When it is acceptable.** For air, this is an excellent approximation below roughly 1000 K (roughly $M_1 < 5$ for normal shocks at standard conditions). Below this temperature, only translational and rotational molecular energy modes are active, and their contribution to $c_v$ is fixed. Above 1000 K, vibrational modes progressively activate, $c_v$ and $c_p$ increase with temperature, and $\gamma$ decreases. At that point the gas is still thermally perfect ($p = \rho R T$ holds) but no longer calorically perfect, and numerical solutions are generally required.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem involves normal shocks below about Mach 5, or flow temperatures below about 1000 K, the calorically perfect gas assumption is safe and you can use the tabulated shock and flow relations directly. If the problem involves hypersonic flow (Mach 5+), temperatures above 1000 K, dissociation, or chemical reactions, $\gamma$ is no longer constant and the closed-form relations break down.

## 22. One-Dimensional Flow

> [!question]
>
> (a) What does it mean for a flow to be one-dimensional?
> (b) What does one-dimensionality imply about the cross-sectional area of the flow?
> (c) How does one-dimensional flow differ from quasi-one-dimensional flow?

> [!assumption] One-Dimensional Flow
>
> All flow properties ($p$, $\rho$, $T$, $u$) are functions of only one spatial coordinate $x$.

**Definition.** In a truly one-dimensional flow, the properties are uniform across every cross-section and vary only in the streamwise direction. This requires a constant cross-sectional area — if the area were changing, conservation of mass would force properties to vary across the section (at minimum, the flow would have a transverse velocity component to follow the changing walls). One-dimensional flow is the simplest model in compressible flow analysis and reduces the integral conservation equations to algebraic relations.

**When it is acceptable.** This is a good model for flow through constant-area ducts, across normal shock waves (which are planar and perpendicular to the flow), and for the core flow in regions where the boundary layers are thin relative to the duct diameter. It breaks down when the duct area varies (that becomes quasi-one-dimensional flow, Chapter 5), when strong cross-sectional gradients exist (boundary layers, separated flow), or when the flow has significant multi-dimensional features.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem describes a constant-area duct, a normal shock wave, or states that properties vary only in one direction, you can apply one-dimensional flow equations directly. If the problem involves varying area, curved ducts, or asks about cross-sectional profiles, the one-dimensional assumption is insufficient.

## 23. Steady Flow

> [!question]
>
> (a) What is the mathematical consequence of assuming steady flow?
> (b) Which terms vanish from the conservation equations?
> (c) What phrases in a problem statement signal that you can assume steady flow?

> [!assumption] Steady Flow
>
> All flow properties at every fixed point in space are constant in time: $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\cdot) = 0$.

**When it is acceptable.** Steady flow is valid when boundary conditions are not changing with time and all transients have died out. Most cruise-condition analyses, fully developed duct flows, and design-point engine calculations are treated as steady. It breaks down during startup transients, in flows with vortex shedding, pulsating flows, or any situation with time-dependent boundary conditions.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** Look for time-derivative terms in the conservation equations — the storage terms $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iiint \rho d\mathscr{V}$, $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\iiint \rho \mathbf{V} d\mathscr{V}$, etc. If the problem states "steady" or describes constant operating conditions, set all $\partial/\partial t$ terms to zero. This eliminates the storage terms and leaves only flux and source terms in the integral equations, or reduces them to the algebraic station-to-station equations of Chapter 3.

## 24. Inviscid Flow (No Viscous Stresses)

> [!question]
>
> (a) What terms are dropped from the conservation equations when inviscid flow is assumed?
> (b) What is the primary governing equation set that results?
> (c) Where does this assumption fail?

> [!assumption] Inviscid Flow
>
> Viscous shear stresses are neglected. The only surface force is pressure: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{viscous}} = 0$, $\dot{W}_{\text{viscous}} = 0$.

**When it is acceptable.** Valid in regions where viscous effects are confined to thin boundary layers that do not significantly affect the bulk flow — the core of a duct, the freestream around a body, or any high-Reynolds-number flow where inertial forces dominate. It breaks down inside boundary layers, in separated or recirculating flow, in wakes, and at solid surfaces where the no-slip condition requires viscous stress. Note that the Rayleigh-line flow equations (heat addition, Section 3.8) are inviscid, whereas the Fanno-line flow equations (friction, Section 3.9) explicitly include the effect of wall shear stress.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem states "inviscid," "frictionless," or "ideal fluid," or if you are analyzing the core flow away from walls, you can drop viscous terms. If the problem involves friction at duct walls (Fanno flow), drag, skin friction, or heat transfer at a wall, viscous effects are central and cannot be dropped.

## 25. No Body Forces

> [!question]
>
> (a) What body force terms are set to zero under this assumption?
> (b) Why is this assumption almost universally valid in gas dynamics?
> (c) When would you need to retain body forces?

> [!assumption] No Body Forces
>
> The body force term is neglected: $\iiint \rho \mathbf{f} d\mathscr{V} = 0$ and $\iiint \rho(\mathbf{f}\cdot\mathbf{V}) d\mathscr{V} = 0$.

**When it is acceptable.** In virtually all gas dynamics problems, the gravitational body force is negligible compared to pressure and inertia forces. The ratio of gravitational to inertia forces scales as $gL/u^2$, which is extremely small for high-speed gas flows. This assumption is used throughout Chapter 3 for all one-dimensional flow analyses.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem involves gas flow at any appreciable speed through ducts, nozzles, or over bodies, you can safely neglect body forces. The only situations where body forces matter in gas dynamics are very large-scale atmospheric flows, buoyancy-driven flows, or flows where gravity drives the motion (e.g., chimney effects).

## 26. Adiabatic Flow

> [!question]
>
> (a) What term is set to zero in the energy equation under the adiabatic assumption?
> (b) What is the key consequence for total temperature?
> (c) Is a normal shock adiabatic? Is Fanno-line flow adiabatic? Is Rayleigh-line flow adiabatic?

> [!assumption] Adiabatic Flow
>
> No heat is transferred into or out of the flow: $\dot{Q} = 0$, or equivalently $q = 0$ per unit mass.

**When it is acceptable.** Valid when the fluid passes through the region of interest so quickly that heat exchange with the surroundings is negligible, or when the boundaries are well-insulated. Normal shock flow is adiabatic (no external heat added to the thin shock structure). Fanno-line flow (flow with friction) is adiabatic by definition — friction converts mechanical energy to thermal energy within the flow but no heat crosses the control surface. Rayleigh-line flow is *not* adiabatic — heat addition is the driving mechanism.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem states "adiabatic," "insulated," or "no heat transfer," you can set $q = 0$. The key consequence is that total enthalpy $h_o$ (and for a calorically perfect gas, $T_o$) is constant: $h_1 + u_1^2/2 = h_2 + u_2^2/2$. If the problem involves combustion, heat exchangers, radiation, or any mechanism that adds or removes thermal energy, the adiabatic assumption does not hold.

## 27. Isentropic Process

> [!question]
>
> (a) What two conditions must both hold for a process to be isentropic?
> (b) Is flow through a normal shock isentropic? Why or why not?
> (c) What relations become available when the flow is isentropic?

> [!assumption] Isentropic Process
>
> The process is both reversible and adiabatic, hence the entropy is constant: $\Delta s = 0$.

**When it is acceptable.** A flow is isentropic when it is adiabatic (no heat addition) and reversible (no friction, no shocks, no other dissipative mechanisms). The flow through a sound wave is isentropic because the disturbance is so weak that dissipation is negligible. Flow outside boundary layers in a smooth nozzle expansion or compression is typically isentropic. Normal shock flow is *not* isentropic — it is adiabatic but irreversible (the large gradients within the shock produce viscous dissipation and thermal conduction). Rayleigh-line flow is not isentropic because it is non-adiabatic. Fanno-line flow is not isentropic because friction makes it irreversible.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem states "isentropic" or describes flow without shocks, friction, or heat addition, you can use the isentropic relations $p v^\gamma = \text{const}$ and the total-to-static ratios (Entries 5–7). Critically, in isentropic flow, the total pressure $p_o$ is constant throughout the flowfield — this is a powerful tool for connecting conditions at different stations.

## 28. Uniform Flow at Each Station

> [!question]
>
> (a) What does this assumption allow you to do with surface integrals?
> (b) How does it convert the integral conservation equations into algebraic equations?
> (c) When does this assumption break down?

> [!assumption] Uniform Flow at Each Station
>
> All flow properties are spatially uniform across each cross-section. The flow may vary from station to station but not within a station.

**When it is acceptable.** This is reasonable when the flow is well-mixed across the cross-section — turbulent duct flow far from the entrance, the core of a nozzle, or any cross-section where boundary layer effects are small relative to the duct diameter. It breaks down near walls in laminar flow, in separated flow regions, or where significant velocity or temperature profiles exist.

**How to recognize when you can apply it.** If the problem gives you a single value of $u$, $p$, $T$, and $\rho$ at each station (rather than profiles), or states "uniform properties," you can replace each surface integral with an algebraic product: $\iint_S \rho \mathbf{V}\cdot d\mathbf{S} \to \rho u A$, etc. This is the step that converts the integral conservation equations from Chapter 2 into the algebraic equations of Chapter 3.

## 29. Fixed Control Volume

> [!question]
>
> (a) What simplification does a fixed control volume provide?
> (b) Is the control volume around a normal shock fixed?

> [!assumption] Fixed Control Volume
>
> The control volume is stationary and non-deforming in the chosen reference frame.

**When it is acceptable.** All the one-dimensional flow analyses in Chapter 3 use a fixed control volume — the rectangular region drawn around the flow in Fig. 3.6. ## 30. Constant-Area Duct

> [!question]
>
> (a) Why does one-dimensional flow require constant area?
> (b) How does variable area change the analysis?

> [!assumption] Constant-Area Duct
>
> The cross-sectional area of the flow is the same at every station: $A_1 = A_2 = A$.

**When it is acceptable.** This is the defining geometric condition of truly one-dimensional flow. The area cancels from all the conservation equations, leaving the algebraic relations of Chapter 3. If the area varies, the flow becomes quasi-one-dimensional (Chapter 5), and the area ratio $A_2/A_1$ enters the equations as an additional parameter.

## 31. Weak Disturbance (Sound Wave)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Why is a sound wave classified as a "weak" disturbance?
> (b) What approximation does this allow in the derivation of the speed of sound?
> (c) What is the strong-disturbance counterpart of a sound wave?

> [!assumption] Weak Disturbance
>
> The changes in flow properties through the wave are infinitesimally small. Products of differentials can be neglected.

**When it is acceptable.** This assumption is used in the derivation of the speed of sound (Section 3.3). The sound wave produces infinitesimal changes $dp$, $d\rho$, $dT$, and $da$. Products like $d\rho \cdot da$ are second-order infinitesimals and are dropped. This is what makes the process through a sound wave isentropic (the gradients are too small for dissipation to matter) and is what leads to the fundamental result $a^2 = (\partial p/\partial \rho)_s$. The strong-disturbance counterpart is a shock wave, where the changes are finite and the process is irreversible.

## 32. Normal Shock Is a Discontinuity

> [!question]
>
> (a) Why is the shock treated as a discontinuity even though it has finite thickness?
> (b) What internal mechanisms are at work inside the shock structure?
> (c) What conservation laws hold across the discontinuity?

> [!assumption] Normal Shock Is a Discontinuity
>
> The normal shock is treated as an infinitely thin surface across which the flow properties change instantaneously. The internal structure of the shock is not resolved.

**When it is acceptable.** The shock thickness is on the order of a few molecular mean free paths ($\sim 10^{-5}$ cm for air at standard conditions). For any macroscopic analysis, this is effectively zero, and treating the shock as a discontinuity is an excellent approximation. Inside the shock, viscosity and thermal conduction produce large gradients and irreversible dissipation — but these details are "inside the black box." The conservation equations for mass, momentum, and energy hold across the discontinuity regardless of the internal structure.

## 33. No Shaft Work

> [!question]
>
> (a) What term is set to zero?
> (b) When does this assumption apply in Chapter 3?

> [!assumption] No Shaft Work
>

**When it is acceptable.** This is assumed throughout Chapter 3 for all one-dimensional flow analyses — normal shocks, heat addition, and friction. The control volumes considered do not contain turbines, compressors, pumps, or other work-producing devices. If the control volume included such machinery (as in a turbomachinery stage), the shaft work term would need to be retained in the energy equation.

## 34. Frictionless Flow (Rayleigh)

> [!question]
>
> (a) What is the specific assumption in Rayleigh-line flow regarding friction?
> (b) What remains as the only mechanism for property changes?
> (c) Why is this a reasonable model for a short combustor?

> [!assumption] Frictionless Flow (Rayleigh-Line)
>
> Wall friction is neglected: the momentum equation has no shear stress term. The only mechanism causing property changes is heat addition.

**When it is acceptable.** This is the key assumption of Rayleigh-line flow (Section 3.8). It is reasonable for short ducts (like combustion chambers) where the friction effects are small compared to the dominant effect of heat addition. For long ducts with both friction and heat addition, neither Rayleigh nor Fanno analysis alone suffices, and a combined treatment is needed.

## 35. Adiabatic Flow with Wall Friction (Fanno)

> [!question]
>
> (a) What combination of assumptions defines Fanno-line flow?
> (b) Why is total temperature constant in Fanno flow even though friction is present?
> (c) What drives the flow property changes?

> [!assumption] Adiabatic Flow with Wall Friction (Fanno-Line)
>
> The flow is adiabatic ($q = 0$) but friction at the duct wall is the mechanism causing property changes. There is no heat addition and no shock waves.

**When it is acceptable.** This is the key assumption of Fanno-line flow (Section 3.9). It models the flow through long, insulated ducts where friction is the dominant effect. Total temperature $T_o$ is constant because the flow is adiabatic — friction converts directed kinetic energy to thermal energy within the fluid, but no energy crosses the control surface. The entropy increases because friction is irreversible, which means total pressure always decreases along the duct.

# One-Dimensional Conservation Equations

## 36. Continuity equation for steady 1-D flow (Eq. 3.2)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the continuity equation for steady one-dimensional flow.
> (b) What fundamental conservation law does this equation express?
> (c) If a gas speeds up between two stations, what must happen to its density, and why?
> (d) Why is it mass flux $\rho u$ (not velocity alone) that is conserved?

> [!equation] Continuity equation for steady 1-D flow (Eq. 3.2)
>
> $$ \rho_{1} u_{1}=\rho_{2} u_{2} $$

This equation expresses the most fundamental conservation law in fluid mechanics: mass cannot be created or destroyed. In a steady, constant-area duct, whatever mass flows in per second must flow out per second. Since the area is the same on both sides, the mass flux $\rho u$ — the mass passing through a unit area per unit time — must be identical at every station. This means density and velocity are locked in an inverse relationship: if the gas speeds up, it must become less dense (it stretches out), and if it slows down, it must become denser (it piles up). Every other one-dimensional flow relation is built on this constraint.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$\rho_1 u_1$|kg/(m²·s)|kg·m⁻²·s⁻¹|**Mass Flux at Station 1**|The mass flow rate per unit area entering the control volume.|
|$\rho_2 u_2$|kg/(m²·s)|kg·m⁻²·s⁻¹|**Mass Flux at Station 2**|The mass flow rate per unit area leaving the control volume.|

## 37. Momentum equation for steady 1-D flow (Eq. 3.5)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the momentum equation for steady one-dimensional flow.
> (b) What does the combination $p + \rho u^2$ represent physically as the total "push" at a cross-section?
> (c) When a gas decelerates through a shock, $\rho u^2$ decreases — where does that momentum go?
> (d) Why can pressure and momentum flux trade back and forth while their sum remains constant?

> [!equation] Momentum equation for steady 1-D flow (Eq. 3.5)
>
> $$ p_{1}+\rho_{1} u_{1}^{2}=p_{2}+\rho_{2} u_{2}^{2} $$

This equation is Newton's second law applied to a steady stream of gas: the total "push" exerted by the flow at any cross-section — combining the pressure force ($p$) and the momentum carried by the moving mass ($\rho u^2$) — must be the same everywhere, because there are no external forces acting between the stations to change it. Pressure and momentum flux can trade back and forth: when the gas decelerates (as behind a shock), $\rho u^2$ decreases but $p$ increases by exactly the same amount, keeping the impulse function $p + \rho u^2$ constant. This conservation of the impulse function is what makes pressure jumps across shocks predictable — the momentum the gas loses in slowing down is converted entirely into a pressure rise.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$p$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Static Pressure**|The pressure force per unit area.|
|$\rho u^2$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Momentum Flux per Unit Area**|The rate of streamwise momentum transport per unit area.|
|$p + \rho u^2$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Impulse Function**|Conserved in steady, inviscid, 1-D flow without body forces.|

## Assumptions and Simplifications

1. **Steady flow.** The time-derivative (storage) term vanishes.
2. **Inviscid flow.** No viscous shear stress terms.
3. **No body forces.** The body-force volume integral is zero.
4. **One-dimensional, constant area.** Properties uniform across each face; area cancels.

## 38. Energy equation for steady 1-D flow (Eq. 3.9)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the energy equation for steady one-dimensional flow with heat addition.
> (b) What does the quantity $h + u^2/2$ represent as the "complete energy per unit mass" of the flow?
> (c) Why does enthalpy $h$ (rather than internal energy $e$) appear — what additional energy does it account for?
> (d) If $q = 0$, what does this equation guarantee about total enthalpy, even through shock waves?

> [!equation] Energy equation for steady 1-D flow (Eq. 3.9)
>
> $$ h_{1}+\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{2}+q=h_{2}+\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{2} $$

This equation is the first law of thermodynamics for a flowing gas: the total energy of the fluid — thermal content plus motion energy — can only change if heat is added or removed. The quantity $h + u^2/2$ is the total enthalpy, representing the complete energy per unit mass that the flow carries. If no heat is exchanged ($q = 0$), total enthalpy is perfectly conserved, even through violent shock waves. If heat is added ($q > 0$, as in combustion), the total enthalpy increases by exactly $q$. Enthalpy $h$ rather than internal energy $e$ appears because a flowing gas must also do "flow work" ($p/\rho$) to push itself through its surroundings — enthalpy bundles this mechanical bookkeeping together with the thermal energy, making it the natural energy variable for open systems.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$h$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Static Enthalpy**|The thermal energy per unit mass including flow work.|
|$u^2/2$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Kinetic Energy per Unit Mass**|The macroscopic motion energy.|
|$q$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Heat Added per Unit Mass**|The thermal energy added between the two stations.|
|$h + u^2/2$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Total Enthalpy**|The total energy per unit mass carried by the flow.|

## Assumptions and Simplifications

1. **Steady flow.** The energy storage term vanishes.
2. **No body forces.** The body-force work integral is zero.
3. **No shaft work.** No mechanical work from machinery.
4. **No viscous work.** Viscous stresses do no work (either inviscid or the viscous work is at stationary walls).
5. **One-dimensional, constant area.** Uniform properties at each face.
6. **Neglected terms.** Heat transfer by thermal conduction and diffusion is not explicitly included; $q$ represents bulk heat addition (e.g., from combustion), not wall conduction.

## 39. Adiabatic energy equation (Eq. 3.21)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the adiabatic energy equation for steady 1-D flow.
> (b) Explain the "fast and cold" vs. "slow and hot" trade-off that this equation describes.
> (c) A gas accelerates through a nozzle — what happens to its temperature, and why?
> (d) Why does this energy conservation hold even across a violent, irreversible shock wave?

> [!equation] Adiabatic energy equation (Eq. 3.21)
>
> $$ h_{1}+\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{2}=h_{2}+\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{2} $$

This equation asserts that in a steady, adiabatic flow where no mechanical work is performed, the total energy of the fluid — comprising its thermal content and flow work (enthalpy) plus its motion (kinetic energy) — is a conserved quantity. It describes a fundamental trade-off: a fluid element can be "fast and cold" or "slow and hot," but it cannot gain speed without sacrificing thermal energy, nor can it warm up without slowing down, so long as no heat is added or removed. This energy conservation holds across normal shocks, through Fanno-line friction flows, and indeed along any streamline in any steady adiabatic flow regardless of dimensionality. It is the foundation upon which all stagnation properties, nozzle flows, and shock analyses are built.

## 40. Adiabatic energy equation — calorically perfect gas (Eq. 3.22)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the energy equation for a calorically perfect gas in adiabatic flow.
> (b) What role does $c_p$ play as the "exchange rate" between temperature changes and velocity changes?
> (c) For a gas with high $c_p$ versus low $c_p$, which experiences smaller temperature swings for the same velocity change?

> [!equation] Adiabatic energy equation — calorically perfect gas (Eq. 3.22)
>
> $$ c_{p} T_{1}+\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{2}=c_{p} T_{2}+\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{2} $$

For a calorically perfect gas, the energy trade-off becomes a direct, quantitative exchange between temperature and velocity: every increase in $u^2/2$ causes a proportional drop in $c_p T$, and every deceleration returns that energy as heat. The constant $c_p$ acts as the exchange rate — it tells you how many degrees of temperature change correspond to a given change in kinetic energy. A gas with high $c_p$ (many internal energy modes) experiences smaller temperature swings for the same velocity change, while a gas with low $c_p$ heats up and cools down more dramatically. This direct $T \leftrightarrow u$ exchange is what makes compressible flow through nozzles, diffusers, and shock waves analytically tractable for perfect gases.

## 41. Energy equation in terms of speed of sound (Eq. 3.24)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the energy equation in terms of the speed of sound squared.
> (b) As a gas accelerates in a nozzle, what happens to its local speed of sound, and what does this mean physically?
> (c) Why does the gas become "slower at transmitting information" as it speeds up?

> [!equation] Energy equation in terms of speed of sound (Eq. 3.24)
>
> $$ \frac{a_{1}^{2}}{\gamma-1}+\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{2}=\frac{a_{2}^{2}}{\gamma-1}+\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{2} $$

This form re-expresses the energy balance using the speed of sound $a$ as a stand-in for temperature. Since $a^2 \propto T$, the term $a^2/(\gamma-1)$ is the thermal energy in disguise, while $u^2/2$ is the kinetic energy. The equation says that as a gas accelerates ($u$ increases), its local speed of sound must decrease ($a$ drops) — the gas literally becomes "slower at transmitting information" as it speeds up, because it is cooling down. This is the physical mechanism behind many compressible flow phenomena: in a converging nozzle, the flow accelerates and cools until $u$ catches up with $a$ at the throat ($M = 1$). In a shock wave, the flow abruptly decelerates, and the freed kinetic energy drives $a$ (and temperature) sharply upward.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$a^2/(\gamma-1)$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Thermal Energy Term**|Proportional to $c_p T$; represents thermal energy per unit mass.|
|$u^2/2$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Kinetic Energy Term**|Kinetic energy per unit mass.|

## 42. Energy equation in terms of $p/\rho$ (Eq. 3.25)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the energy equation using $p/\rho$ instead of $T$ or $a$.
> (b) What does the ratio $p/\rho$ physically represent as a measure of thermal-pressure energy per unit mass?
> (c) As a gas accelerates and $u^2/2$ increases, what must happen to $p/\rho$?

> [!equation] Energy equation in terms of $p/\rho$ (Eq. 3.25)
>
> $$ \frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{p_{1}}{\rho_{1}}\right)+\frac{u_{1}^{2}}{2}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\left(\frac{p_{2}}{\rho_{2}}\right)+\frac{u_{2}^{2}}{2} $$

This form expresses the energy balance directly in terms of the mechanical variables pressure and density, without explicit reference to temperature. The quantity $\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]$ is the enthalpy written in a form that emphasizes its mechanical origin: it is the pressure energy per unit mass, amplified by the factor $\gamma/(\gamma-1)$ that accounts for the gas's compressibility. This version is physically intuitive for understanding what happens in a duct or shock: as a gas accelerates and its kinetic energy $u^2/2$ increases, the ratio $p/\rho$ — a measure of the thermal-pressure energy per unit mass — must decrease proportionally.

## 43. Energy equation with stagnation speed of sound (Eq. 3.32)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the energy equation in terms of the stagnation speed of sound $a_o$.
> (b) What does $a_o$ represent physically as the "maximum thermal state" of the gas?
> (c) What is the physical meaning of the ratio $a^*/a_o$, and what is its numerical value for air?

> [!equation] Energy equation with stagnation speed of sound (Eq. 3.32)
>
> $$ \frac{a^{2}}{\gamma-1}+\frac{u^{2}}{2}=\frac{a_{o}^{2}}{\gamma-1} $$

The stagnation speed of sound $a_o$ is the speed of sound the gas would have if brought completely to rest. It represents the maximum possible thermal state of the gas — the state where all kinetic energy has been returned to thermal form. The right-hand side $a_o^2/(\gamma-1)$ is therefore the total enthalpy expressed as a single velocity-squared quantity, providing a ceiling: $a^2/(\gamma-1) + u^2/2$ can never exceed $a_o^2/(\gamma-1)$ because you cannot create energy from nothing. The relationship $a^*/a_o = \sqrt{2/(\gamma+1)}$ reveals that the sonic reference speed is always a fixed fraction of the stagnation speed — for air, $a^* \approx 0.913\, a_o$.

# Normal Shock Relations

## 44. Prandtl relation (Eq. 3.47)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the Prandtl relation for normal shocks.
> (b) What does it mean physically that the upstream and downstream velocities are "mirror images" about $a^*$?
> (c) Why does $u_1 u_2 = a^{*2}$ guarantee that a normal shock always converts supersonic flow to subsonic?
> (d) What is the equivalent statement in terms of characteristic Mach numbers ($M_1^* M_2^* = 1$)?

> [!equation] Prandtl relation (Eq. 3.47)
>
> $$ a^{* 2}=u_{1} u_{2} $$

The Prandtl relation reveals the deep symmetry of a normal shock: the upstream and downstream velocities are "mirror images" of each other about the sonic reference $a^*$. Their product is always $a^{*2}$, meaning if the incoming flow is faster than $a^*$, the outgoing flow must be slower by exactly the reciprocal factor, and vice versa. This is the fundamental reason why a normal shock always converts supersonic flow to subsonic flow — one velocity must be above $a^*$ and the other below, with the shock acting as the enforcer of this geometric-mean constraint. The relation encodes all three conservation laws (mass, momentum, and energy) in a single, compact statement. It is also the gateway to every other normal shock relation: once you know $u_1 u_2 = a^{*2}$, all the property ratios follow.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$u_1$|m/s|m·s⁻¹|**Upstream Velocity**|The flow velocity ahead of the shock.|
|$u_2$|m/s|m·s⁻¹|**Downstream Velocity**|The flow velocity behind the shock.|
|$a^*$|m/s|m·s⁻¹|**Critical Speed of Sound**|The speed of sound at the hypothetical Mach 1 condition; same on both sides of the adiabatic shock.|
|$a^{*2} = u_1 u_2$|m²/s²|m²·s⁻²|**Prandtl Relation**|The geometric-mean constraint linking the upstream and downstream velocities through the shock thermodynamics.|

## Simplifications

An immediate consequence is $M_1^* M_2^* = 1$: the characteristic Mach numbers are reciprocals of each other across the shock. Since $M_1 > 1$ implies $M_1^* > 1$, it follows that $M_2^* < 1$ and hence $M_2 < 1$ — the flow behind a normal shock is always subsonic.

## 45. Mach number behind a normal shock (Eq. 3.51)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the expression for the downstream Mach number as a function of $M_1$.
> (b) What is $M_2$ when $M_1 = 1$? When $M_1 \to \infty$ (for air)?
> (c) Why does $M_2$ approach a floor ($\approx 0.378$ for air) rather than zero as $M_1 \to \infty$? What physical limit prevents $M_2$ from reaching zero?
> (d) Why does $M_2$ depend only on $M_1$ and $\gamma$ — no other flow details?

> [!equation] Mach number behind a normal shock (Eq. 3.51)
>
> $$ M_{2}^{2}=\frac{1+[(\gamma-1) / 2] M_{1}^{2}}{\gamma M_{1}^{2}-(\gamma-1) / 2} $$

This equation answers the central question of normal shock theory: given a supersonic flow hitting a shock, how subsonic does it become? The answer depends only on $M_1$ and $\gamma$ — no other flow details matter. The downstream Mach number is always subsonic ($M_2 < 1$) for any $M_1 > 1$, confirming that a normal shock is nature's mechanism for enforcing the transition from supersonic to subsonic flow. At $M_1 = 1$, $M_2 = 1$ — the shock vanishes into an infinitesimally weak Mach wave. As $M_1 \to \infty$, $M_2$ approaches a floor of $\sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma} \approx 0.378$ for air rather than zero, because even an infinitely strong shock cannot compress the gas beyond the density limit $\rho_2/\rho_1 = (\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$, which keeps the downstream velocity (and thus $M_2$) above a finite minimum.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$M_1$|—|—|**Upstream Mach Number**|The Mach number ahead of the shock; must be $\geq 1$.|
|$M_2$|—|—|**Downstream Mach Number**|The Mach number behind the shock; always subsonic ($< 1$) for $M_1 > 1$.|

## Simplifications

| Limit | $M_2$ | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $M_1 = 1$ | $M_2 = 1$ | Infinitely weak shock (Mach wave); no finite changes. |
| $M_1 \to \infty$ | $M_2 \to \sqrt{(\gamma-1)/2\gamma}$ | Strongest possible shock; $M_2 = 0.378$ for air. |

## 46. Density (and velocity) ratio across a normal shock (Eq. 3.53)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the density and velocity ratio across a normal shock.
> (b) What is the maximum density ratio as $M_1 \to \infty$?
> (c) Why are the density and velocity ratios reciprocals of each other?

> [!equation] Density (and velocity) ratio across a normal shock (Eq. 3.53)
>
> $$ \frac{\rho_{2}}{\rho_{1}}=\frac{u_{1}}{u_{2}}=\frac{(\gamma+1) M_{1}^{2}}{2+(\gamma-1) M_{1}^{2}} $$

This equation reveals a fundamental physical limit on shock compression: no matter how strong the shock, the gas can only be compressed by a factor of $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1)$ — which is 6 for air. This ceiling exists because as the shock strengthens, the downstream temperature rises without bound (absorbing more and more of the incoming kinetic energy as heat), and this rising temperature acts against further compression through the equation of state ($p = \rho RT$). The velocity ratio is the reciprocal of the density ratio (from mass conservation): if the gas is compressed by a factor of 4, the flow must slow to 1/4 of its upstream speed. This mutual constraint between density and velocity is the continuity equation at work — matter cannot pile up or disappear at the shock.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$\rho_2/\rho_1$|—|—|**Density Ratio**|The ratio of downstream to upstream density; always $\geq 1$.|
|$u_1/u_2$|—|—|**Velocity Ratio**|The reciprocal of the density ratio, from continuity.|

## Simplifications

| Limit | $\rho_2/\rho_1$ | Physical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $M_1 = 1$ | 1 | Mach wave; no compression. |
| $M_1 \to \infty$ | $(\gamma+1)/(\gamma-1) = 6$ (air) | Maximum compression ratio for a normal shock. |

## 47. Static pressure ratio across a normal shock (Eq. 3.57)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the static pressure ratio across a normal shock.
> (b) What is the pressure ratio when $M_1 = 1$? When $M_1 \to \infty$?
> (c) Why does the pressure ratio grow without bound while the density ratio is bounded?

> [!equation] Static pressure ratio across a normal shock (Eq. 3.57)
>
> $$ \frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}=1+\frac{2 \gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_{1}^{2}-1\right) $$

The pressure jump across a normal shock is the most viscerally felt consequence of a shock wave — it is the "bang" of a sonic boom, the force that loads structures, and the primary measure of shock strength. Unlike the density ratio (which has a ceiling of 6 for air), the pressure ratio grows without limit as the shock strengthens. This unbounded growth occurs because both density *and* temperature increase behind the shock, and pressure is the product of both ($p = \rho RT$). Even though density saturates, temperature does not — the incoming kinetic energy is so vast at high $M_1$ that it drives the post-shock temperature (and therefore pressure) sky-high. At $M_1 = 1$ the pressure ratio is unity (no shock), while at $M_1 = 3$ it reaches about 10.3 for air — a tenfold pressure amplification.

## 48. Temperature ratio across a normal shock (Eq. 3.59)

> (a) Write the temperature ratio across a normal shock.
> (b) Physically, what process inside the shock causes the temperature to rise — what happens to the molecular motion?
> (c) Why does the temperature ratio grow without bound as $M_1 \to \infty$, even though the density ratio saturates?
> (d) At approximately what upstream Mach number does the post-shock temperature exceed 1000 K (for standard air), and why does that matter?

The temperature jump across a shock represents the irreversible conversion of directed kinetic energy into random thermal motion. Behind the shock, molecules that were streaming supersonically in one direction have been violently disrupted into disordered, high-speed motion in all directions — this randomization *is* the temperature rise. The temperature ratio grows without bound as $M_1 \to \infty$ because there is no limit to how much kinetic energy can be converted to heat. ## 49. Entropy change across a normal shock (Eq. 3.60)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the entropy change across a normal shock.
> (b) Why is the second law the proof that only compression shocks are physical?
> (c) What happens to $s_2 - s_1$ when $M_1 < 1$ is substituted?

> [!equation] Entropy change across a normal shock (Eq. 3.60)
>
> $$ s_{2}-s_{1}= c_{p} \ln \left\{\left[1+\frac{2 \gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_{1}^{2}-1\right)\right]\left[\frac{2+(\gamma-1) M_{1}^{2}}{(\gamma+1) M_{1}^{2}}\right]\right\} -R \ln \left[1+\frac{2 \gamma}{\gamma+1}\left(M_{1}^{2}-1\right)\right] $$

The entropy change across a shock is nature's verdict on whether the shock can exist. A positive $\Delta s$ means the shock is physically realizable; a negative $\Delta s$ would violate the second law and is forbidden. For $M_1 > 1$ (compression shock), $\Delta s > 0$ — the enormous velocity and temperature gradients inside the thin shock structure generate irreversible viscous dissipation and heat conduction, permanently degrading the energy quality of the gas. For $M_1 < 1$ (hypothetical expansion shock), the formula yields $\Delta s < 0$, which is impossible — this is the thermodynamic proof that expansion shocks do not exist in nature. At $M_1 = 1$, $\Delta s = 0$: the disturbance is infinitesimally weak (a Mach wave), and no irreversibility occurs. The stronger the shock (higher $M_1$), the greater the entropy production and the more energy is irreversibly lost.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$s_2 - s_1$|J/(kg·K)|m²·s⁻²·K⁻¹|**Entropy Change**|Always $\geq 0$ for physically realizable shocks ($M_1 \geq 1$).|

## 50. Total temperature is constant across a normal shock (Eq. 3.61)

A normal shock, despite being a violent and irreversible event, does not change the total temperature of the flow. This degradation shows up as an entropy increase and a total pressure loss — but the total temperature, being a pure measure of energy content, remains untouched. ## 51. Entropy change in terms of total pressure ratio (Eq. 3.63)

This equation makes explicit the intimate connection between entropy and total pressure in adiabatic flows: every bit of entropy produced by an irreversible process directly destroys total pressure. An engineer measuring the total pressure drop across a shock, a turbine, or a length of duct is directly measuring the thermodynamic damage inflicted by irreversibility. This is why total pressure recovery is the universal metric of aerodynamic efficiency.

## 52. Total pressure ratio across a normal shock (Eq. 3.64)

> [!question]

> (a) Write the total pressure ratio across a normal shock.
> (b) What determines the magnitude of the total pressure loss?

>
> $$ \frac{p_{o_{2}}}{p_{o_{1}}}=e^{-\left(s_{2}-s_{1}\right) / R} $$

This ratio is the report card of a shock wave: it tells you what fraction of the flow's work-producing potential survives the shock. This dramatic sensitivity to $M_1$ is why supersonic inlet designers go to extraordinary lengths to avoid strong normal shocks, instead using systems of weaker oblique shocks that each produce less entropy. The exponential dependence on entropy means that small reductions in irreversibility yield large gains in total pressure recovery.

## 53. Hugoniot equation (Eq. 3.72)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the Hugoniot equation.
> (b) Why is this equation "purely thermodynamic" — what has been removed, and what remains?
> (c) What does it mean that the Hugoniot defines the locus of all possible downstream states from a given upstream state?
> (d) Is this equation limited to perfect gases, or does it hold for any gas?

> [!equation] Hugoniot equation (Eq. 3.72)
>
> $$ e_{2}-e_{1}=\frac{p_{1}+p_{2}}{2}\left(v_{1}-v_{2}\right) $$

The Hugoniot equation strips the shock wave down to its thermodynamic essence: it relates the internal energy change to the pressure–volume work done during the compression, with all reference to flow velocity removed. What remains is a purely thermodynamic statement about the two equilibrium states on either side of the shock. The equation says that the increase in internal energy ($e_2 - e_1$) equals the average pressure times the decrease in specific volume — physically, the gas gains thermal energy by being compressed under the mean of the upstream and downstream pressures. This result is remarkable for its generality: it holds for *any* gas, whether perfect, reacting, or real, because it is built only from the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy with no assumptions about the gas's equation of state. The Hugoniot defines the locus of all possible downstream states achievable by a shock from a given upstream state.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$e$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Specific Internal Energy**|The microscopic thermal energy per unit mass.|
|$v = 1/\rho$|m³/kg|m³·kg⁻¹|**Specific Volume**|The volume per unit mass; the reciprocal of density.|
|$(p_1+p_2)/2$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Mean Pressure**|The average of the upstream and downstream static pressures.|

## 54. Hugoniot equation for a calorically perfect gas

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the Hugoniot equation specialized to a calorically perfect gas.
> (b) How does the Hugoniot curve compare to the isentrope on a $p$–$v$ diagram?

> [!equation] Hugoniot equation — calorically perfect gas
>
> $$ \frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}=\frac{\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\right) \frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}}-1}{\left(\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}\right)-\frac{v_{1}}{v_{2}}} $$

For a perfect gas, the Hugoniot takes a closed-form that maps out every possible downstream state on a $p$–$v$ diagram. The Hugoniot curve always lies above the isentrope through the same starting point, and the gap between them widens with shock strength. This gap is the visual signature of irreversibility: a shock achieves a given compression ratio with a *higher* pressure rise than an isentropic process would, because the entropy produced inside the shock adds extra thermal energy (and thus extra pressure) beyond what reversible compression would produce. For weak shocks, the Hugoniot and isentrope are virtually indistinguishable — the shock is nearly reversible. For strong shocks, the curves diverge dramatically, reflecting the large thermodynamic penalty of irreversible compression. The Hugoniot also reveals the maximum compression limit $v_2/v_1 \to (\gamma-1)/(\gamma+1)$ where the curve has a vertical asymptote.

## 55. Hugoniot curve slope — Rayleigh line (Eq. 3.75)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the expression for the slope of the Rayleigh line connecting states 1 and 2 on the Hugoniot curve.
> (b) How do you use this to find the downstream state for a given upstream velocity?

> [!equation] Hugoniot curve slope (Eq. 3.75)
>
> $$ \frac{p_{2}-p_{1}}{v_{2}-v_{1}}=-\left(\frac{u_{1}}{v_{1}}\right)^{2} $$

This equation gives a geometric interpretation of the shock process on a $p$–$v$ diagram. The downstream state is found where a straight line (the Rayleigh line) through the upstream point intersects the Hugoniot curve, and the slope of that line is set by the mass flux $\rho_1 u_1 = u_1/v_1$. Physically, the Rayleigh line embodies the constraints of mass and momentum conservation: only states along this line satisfy both. The Hugoniot curve embodies the energy constraint. The shock must satisfy all three simultaneously, so it must lie at the intersection. A faster incoming flow (higher $u_1$) steepens the Rayleigh line, pushing the intersection to higher pressures and lower volumes — a stronger shock. This graphical construction provides deep insight into why certain shock strengths are possible and others are not.

# One-Dimensional Flow with Heat Addition — Rayleigh Line

## 56. Heat addition and total temperature change (Eq. 3.77)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the equation relating heat addition to the change in total temperature.
> (b) Why is total temperature the *only* flow property that responds to heat addition in a simple, one-to-one manner?
> (c) If you know $q$ and $T_{o_1}$, how do you immediately find $T_{o_2}$ — and why is this so powerful as a starting point for Rayleigh-line problems?

> [!equation] Heat addition and total temperature change (Eq. 3.77)
>
> $$ q=c_{p}\left(T_{o_{2}}-T_{o_{1}}\right) $$

This equation reveals that heat addition has a uniquely clean effect on total temperature: every joule of heat added per kilogram raises $T_o$ by exactly $1/c_p$ degrees, regardless of whether the flow is subsonic or supersonic, and regardless of how the Mach number, pressure, or velocity change. Total temperature is the *only* flow property that responds to heat addition in such a simple, one-to-one manner — all other properties (static temperature, pressure, density, velocity) change in complicated ways that depend on the local Mach number. This simplicity makes $T_o$ the natural "currency" for heat addition problems: you always know the exit total temperature immediately from $T_{o_2} = T_{o_1} + q/c_p$, and all other exit properties follow from there.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$q$|J/kg|m²·s⁻²|**Heat Added per Unit Mass**|The thermal energy added between the two stations.|
|$T_{o_1}$|K|K|**Inlet Total Temperature**|The stagnation temperature at the inlet.|
|$T_{o_2}$|K|K|**Exit Total Temperature**|The stagnation temperature at the exit; $T_{o_2} = T_{o_1} + q/c_p$.|

## 57. Static pressure ratio — heat addition (Eq. 3.78)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the pressure ratio for 1-D flow with heat addition.
> (b) What does the quantity $1 + \gamma M^2$ represent physically as a normalized impulse function?
> (c) Heat addition *decreases* pressure in subsonic flow but *increases* pressure in supersonic flow — explain this asymmetry physically.
> (d) In both cases, heat addition drives the Mach number toward 1 — why?

> [!equation] Static pressure ratio — Rayleigh-line flow (Eq. 3.78)
>
> $$ \frac{p_{2}}{p_{1}}=\frac{1+\gamma M_{1}^{2}}{1+\gamma M_{2}^{2}} $$

This equation captures a counterintuitive asymmetry: heat addition affects pressure *oppositely* depending on whether the flow is subsonic or supersonic. In subsonic flow, adding heat *decreases* the static pressure (the gas expands and accelerates, dropping the pressure). In supersonic flow, adding heat *increases* the static pressure (the gas decelerates and compresses). The quantity $1 + \gamma M^2$ is the impulse function normalized by $p$ — it is constant between the two stations because the momentum equation still holds (heat addition exerts no force on the gas). Both the subsonic and supersonic branches of Rayleigh-line flow are driven toward $M = 1$ by heat addition: subsonic flow accelerates toward sonic, while supersonic flow decelerates toward sonic.

|**Expression**|**Units**|**Base Units**|**Name**|**Description**|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|$1 + \gamma M^2$|—|—|**Normalized Impulse Function**|The ratio $(p + \rho u^2)/p$; conserved in the momentum equation for 1-D frictionless flow.|

## 58. Static temperature ratio — heat addition (Eq. 3.81)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the temperature ratio for Rayleigh-line flow.
> (b) In subsonic flow, adding heat can actually *decrease* the static temperature above $M \approx 0.845$ — explain this counterintuitive result physically.
> (c) Why does heat addition always *increase* the static temperature in supersonic flow?
> (d) What is the critical Mach number ($1/\sqrt{\gamma}$) where the static temperature behavior changes?

> [!equation] Static temperature ratio — Rayleigh-line flow (Eq. 3.81)
>
> $$ \frac{T_{2}}{T_{1}}=\left(\frac{1+\gamma M_{1}^{2}}{1+\gamma M_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}}\right)^{2} $$

The static temperature in Rayleigh-line flow can behave in surprising ways. For subsonic flow, adding heat initially raises the static temperature (as expected), but beyond a critical Mach number ($M = 1/\sqrt{\gamma} \approx 0.845$ for air) the static temperature actually *decreases* even as more heat is added — the gas is accelerating so rapidly toward $M = 1$ that the kinetic energy increase outpaces the heat input, drawing down the thermal reservoir. For supersonic flow, heat addition always increases the static temperature because the flow is decelerating (releasing kinetic energy back to thermal form) at the same time heat is being added. This non-monotonic behavior in the subsonic regime is one of the most unintuitive results in compressible flow.

## 59. Total pressure ratio — heat addition (Eq. 3.83)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the total pressure ratio for Rayleigh-line flow.
> (b) Why does heat addition *always* decrease total pressure, whether the flow is subsonic or supersonic?
> (c) What is the fundamental design tension in a jet engine combustor between high temperature and total pressure preservation?
> (d) Why do engine designers burn fuel at the lowest possible Mach number?

> [!equation] Total pressure ratio — Rayleigh-line flow (Eq. 3.83)
>
> $$ \frac{p_{o_{2}}}{p_{o_{1}}}=\frac{1+\gamma M_{1}^{2}}{1+\gamma M_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_{2}^{2}}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_{1}^{2}}\right)^{\gamma /(\gamma-1)} $$

Heat addition always destroys total pressure — this is an inescapable thermodynamic cost of combustion or any other heat-transfer process in a flowing gas. Whether the flow enters the heating section subsonically or supersonically, $p_{o_2} < p_{o_1}$. Physically, adding heat increases the entropy of the gas (the energy is introduced in a disordered thermal form), and entropy increase is synonymous with total pressure loss. In a jet engine, the combustor is the primary source of total pressure degradation — the hotter you burn, the more thrust potential you sacrifice to entropy. This creates a fundamental design tension: you need high combustion temperatures for high specific thrust, but every degree of heating costs you total pressure. Engine designers minimize this penalty by burning at the lowest possible Mach number (ideally well below $M = 1$) and keeping the duct short.

## 60. Total temperature ratio — heat addition (Eq. 3.84)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the total temperature ratio for Rayleigh-line flow.
> (b) When heat is added, the energy goes partly into changing static temperature and partly into changing velocity — what governs the split?
> (c) At low Mach numbers, where does most of the added heat go? Near $M = 1$?

> [!equation] Total temperature ratio — Rayleigh-line flow (Eq. 3.84)
>
> $$ \frac{T_{o_{2}}}{T_{o_{1}}}=\left(\frac{1+\gamma M_{1}^{2}}{1+\gamma M_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{M_{2}}{M_{1}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_{2}^{2}}{1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M_{1}^{2}}\right) $$

This equation encodes the complete thermodynamic coupling between heat addition and Mach number change. Although $T_{o_2}/T_{o_1}$ is set directly by the amount of heat added (Entry 56), the way that total temperature change manifests in the flow is complex: the energy goes partly into changing the static temperature and partly into changing the velocity, with the split governed by the local Mach number. At low Mach numbers, most of the heat goes into raising the static temperature; near $M = 1$, an increasing fraction goes into accelerating (or decelerating) the flow. This equation captures that full coupling, relating the total temperature ratio to the Mach numbers at both stations through the interplay of momentum conservation and the equation of state.

## 61. Rayleigh-line flow relations — sonic reference (Eqs. 3.85–3.89)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the Rayleigh-line property ratios referenced to sonic ($M = 1$) conditions.
> (b) Why are the starred quantities constant for a given one-dimensional flow with heat addition?
> (c) How do you use these ratios to solve problems?

> [!equation] Rayleigh-line flow relations (Eqs. 3.85–3.89)
>
> $$ \begin{aligned}
> \frac{p}{p^{*}} & =\frac{1+\gamma}{1+\gamma M^{2}} \\[6pt]
> \frac{T}{T^{*}} & =M^{2}\left(\frac{1+\gamma}{1+\gamma M^{2}}\right)^{2} \\[6pt]
> \frac{\rho}{\rho^{*}} & =\frac{1}{M^{2}}\left(\frac{1+\gamma M^{2}}{1+\gamma}\right) \\[6pt]
> \frac{p_{o}}{p_{o}^{*}} & =\frac{1+\gamma}{1+\gamma M^{2}}\left[\frac{2+(\gamma-1) M^{2}}{\gamma+1}\right]^{\gamma /(\gamma-1)} \\[6pt]
> \frac{T_{o}}{T_{o}^{*}} & =\frac{(\gamma+1) M^{2}}{\left(1+\gamma M^{2}\right)^{2}}\left[2+(\gamma-1) M^{2}\right]
> \end{aligned} $$

These ratios express every local property as a fraction of what that property would be at the hypothetical station where heat addition has driven the flow to exactly $M = 1$. The sonic condition acts as a universal reference point: for a given flow, the starred values ($p^*$, $T^*$, $T_o^*$, etc.) are constants — they represent the endpoint of the Rayleigh-line process, the maximum heat that can be absorbed before the flow chokes. Each ratio is a function of Mach number alone, so they serve as a "lookup table" connecting local conditions to the sonic benchmark. Physically, these ratios reveal how each property evolves as heat is progressively added: pressure and density move in one direction, temperature and velocity in potentially surprising directions (Entry 58), all converging toward their starred values at $M = 1$.

**Important note.** The starred quantities here are fundamentally different from the $T^*$, $p^*$ defined in Section 3.4 (which refer to adiabatically adjusting flow to Mach 1). Here, $T^*$, $p^*$, etc., are the conditions that would exist if enough *heat* were added (a non-adiabatic process) to bring the flow to Mach 1.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$p^*$, $T^*$, $\rho^*$|various|various|**Sonic Reference Conditions**|The static conditions at the station where $M = 1$ due to heat addition; constant for a given flow.|
|$p_o^*$, $T_o^*$|various|various|**Sonic Reference Total Conditions**|The total conditions at the $M = 1$ station; constant for a given flow.|

## 62. Thermal choking condition

> [!question]
>
> (a) What does thermal choking mean?
> (b) What happens if more heat is added after the flow is choked?
> (c) How does nature adjust for excess heat in the supersonic vs. subsonic cases?

> [!equation] Thermal choking
>
> $$ M_{2} = 1 \quad \text{(maximum heat addition)} $$

Thermal choking occurs when enough heat has been added to drive the exit Mach number to exactly 1. At this point, the flow is said to be choked because no additional heat can be accommodated without a drastic change in the upstream conditions. If additional heat is added beyond the choking limit: for an initially supersonic inlet, a normal shock forms upstream (e.g., inside the nozzle that produced the supersonic flow), converting the inlet to subsonic with a lower Mach number that can accept the extra heat. For an initially subsonic inlet, pressure waves propagate upstream and reduce the inlet Mach number to accommodate the added heat. In both cases, nature adjusts so that the exit remains at $M = 1$.

The choking heat $q^*$ is found from $q^* = c_p(T_o^* - T_{o_1})$, where $T_o^*$ is the total temperature at the sonic condition.

# One-Dimensional Flow with Friction — Fanno Line

## 63. Differential momentum equation with friction (Eq. 3.95)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the differential momentum equation for 1-D flow with friction.
> (b) What is the physical tug-of-war between pressure, inertia, and wall friction that this equation describes?
> (c) Why does a narrower duct (smaller $D$) cause friction to "bite harder" on the flow?
> (d) Friction accelerates subsonic flow but decelerates supersonic flow — explain this paradox physically.

> [!equation] Differential momentum equation with friction (Eq. 3.95)
>
> $$ dp+\rho u\, du=-\frac{1}{2} \rho u^{2} \frac{4f\, dx}{D} $$

This equation describes the tug-of-war between pressure, inertia, and wall friction in a duct. On the left, $dp$ and $\rho u\, du$ represent the changes in pressure force and momentum flux — the flow's internal mechanical budget. On the right, friction at the wall steadily drains momentum from the bulk flow. The factor $4f/D$ captures the geometry: a narrower duct (smaller $D$) has more wall surface area per unit of flow volume, so friction bites harder. The equation is differential (not algebraic) because friction acts continuously along the duct — the flow properties change gradually from station to station, unlike a shock where they change abruptly. In subsonic Fanno flow, friction accelerates the gas (pressure drops to push the flow forward against the friction drag); in supersonic Fanno flow, friction decelerates the gas (the wall shear overwhelms the pressure gradient).

|$\rho u\, du$|Pa|kg·m⁻¹·s⁻²|**Momentum Flux Change**|The incremental change in $\rho u^2$ (using continuity: $d(\rho u^2) = \rho u\, du$).|
|$4f\, dx/D$|—|—|**Friction Parameter**|The dimensionless friction force acting over the elemental length $dx$.|

> (b) How do these differ from the Rayleigh-line starred quantities?
> (c) Why is there no $T_o/T_o^*$ ratio for Fanno flow?

These ratios express every local property as a fraction of what that property would be at the station where friction has driven the flow to exactly $M = 1$. Just as in Rayleigh-line flow, the sonic condition serves as the universal reference — but here it is reached through friction rather than heat addition, so the starred values are numerically different. A key distinction: there is no $T_o/T_o^*$ ratio because Fanno flow is adiabatic, meaning $T_o$ is the same everywhere — it never changes regardless of how much friction the flow encounters. Friction cannot add or remove energy; it can only redistribute existing energy between thermal and kinetic forms while irreversibly increasing entropy. The total pressure ratio $p_o/p_o^*$ is always $\geq 1$ (total pressure decreases toward the sonic point), confirming that friction always degrades the flow's work-producing potential.

## 65. Fanno-line friction length relation (Eq. 3.107)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the relation between the friction parameter $4\bar{f}L^*/D$ and Mach number.
> (b) What does $L^*$ represent physically as the "friction budget" remaining before the flow chokes?
> (c) Why does a Mach number far from unity have a large friction budget, while a Mach number near unity has almost none?
> (d) What happens if the physical duct is longer than the available friction budget — how does nature respond in the subsonic vs. supersonic cases?

> [!equation] Fanno-line friction length relation (Eq. 3.107)
>

This equation answers the practical question: "How long a duct does it take for friction to choke this flow?" The dimensionless group $4\bar{f}L^*/D$ represents the total friction "budget" — the cumulative friction resistance from the current station to the hypothetical sonic point. A high Mach number (far from sonic) has a large friction budget (the flow can tolerate a long duct before choking), while a Mach number near unity has almost no budget left (even a short additional duct will choke the flow). The parameter $4\bar{f}/D$ captures the duct's friction intensity: rougher walls (larger $f$) or narrower ducts (smaller $D$) consume the friction budget faster. If the physical duct length exceeds the available friction budget, the flow cannot sustain itself — nature responds by forming a shock (supersonic case) or reducing the inlet Mach number (subsonic case) to create a flow state with a sufficient friction budget.

## 66. Friction choking condition

> [!question]
>
> (a) What does friction choking mean?
> (b) What happens if the duct is longer than the choking length?
> (c) Can friction transition a flow from supersonic to subsonic?

> [!equation] Friction choking
>
> $$ M_{\text{exit}} = 1 \quad \text{(when } L = L^* \text{)} $$

Friction choking occurs when the duct is exactly long enough for friction to drive the exit Mach number to 1. If the duct is made longer than $L^*$, the flow cannot remain as computed — nature adjusts the upstream conditions. For initially supersonic flow, a normal shock forms inside the duct (or upstream in the nozzle), converting the flow to subsonic. For initially subsonic flow, pressure waves propagate upstream and reduce the inlet Mach number. Unlike Rayleigh-line flow, friction *cannot* transition a flow from the supersonic branch to the subsonic branch of the Fanno curve — such a transition would require entropy to decrease, violating the second law.

## 67. Temperature ratio — friction, station-to-station (Eq. 3.98)

> [!question]
>
> (a) Write the temperature ratio between two stations in Fanno-line flow.
> (b) Friction heats a supersonic gas but cools a subsonic gas — explain this paradox physically.
> (c) In supersonic Fanno flow, where does the thermal energy come from as the gas heats up? In subsonic Fanno flow, where does the thermal energy go as the gas cools?
> (d) Why is total temperature constant throughout Fanno flow even though the static temperature changes?

> [!equation] Temperature ratio — Fanno-line flow (Eq. 3.98)
>
> $$ \frac{T_{2}}{T_{1}}=\frac{2+(\gamma-1) M_{1}^{2}}{2+(\gamma-1) M_{2}^{2}} $$

In Fanno flow, the temperature change between two stations is governed purely by how the Mach number changes, because total temperature is constant (the flow is adiabatic). This leads to another counterintuitive result: friction can either heat or cool the gas depending on the flow regime. In supersonic flow, friction decelerates the gas, and the kinetic energy released by the slowing flow is converted into thermal energy — the gas heats up. In subsonic flow, friction paradoxically accelerates the gas (the pressure drop that friction induces pushes the flow faster), and this acceleration draws thermal energy into kinetic form — the gas cools down. In both cases, friction drives the Mach number toward unity and the temperature toward $T^*$, the sonic reference temperature.

|---|---|---|---|---|
|$T_1$, $T_2$|K|K|**Static Temperatures**|The actual temperatures at stations 1 and 2.|
|$M_1$, $M_2$|—|—|**Mach Numbers**|The Mach numbers at stations 1 and 2.|
