## Chapter 1

## Nonlinear Hyperbolic Flow and Characteristic Structure

### 1.1 From Linear to Nonlinear Hyperbolic Structure

In Module 4, we encountered hyperbolic behavior in the context of the small-disturbance compressible potential equation. In that setting, the governing equation was linear with constant coefficients, and its classification as hyperbolic immediately revealed the existence of preferred directions along which disturbances propagate. These directions were fixed, determined entirely by the freestream Mach number, and the solution could be written explicitly in terms of functions of characteristic variables.

The situation we now consider is fundamentally different. We will study steady compressible flows in which the governing equations are nonlinear. As a result, the directions along which information propagates are no longer known a priori. Instead, they depend on the local flow state, which is itself part of the solution. The geometry of propagation and the evolution of the flow are therefore inseparable: to determine how the flow evolves, we must simultaneously determine the directions along which that evolution occurs.

This shift marks a transition from a setting in which characteristic directions can be identified in advance, to one in which they must be computed as part of the solution. This change is fundamental to our solution strategy. We could, in principle, use the same machinery as in Module 4 and write the governing equations in terms of a velocity potential. But in this setting, where the solutions propagate along curved directions, the governing equations would be nonlinear. More than that, the curves of direction propagation depend directly on native flow state variables, such as velocity and density. Finally, we want to be able to consider inviscid flows with rotationality, where a potential flow representation breaks down. It will serve our purposes, therefore, to work directly with the governing equations in velocity-pressure-density variables, rather than in terms of a velocity potential. Make no mistake: the same hyperbolic structure we found in the velocity potential setting persists when working with the primitive flow variables. We will rediscover this fact and find that direct analogs of the PDE characterization process we applied to the velocity potential equations will be useful in this module as well.

Our goal in this chapter is to develop the mathematical framework needed to identify these directions and to understand their role in the structure of the governing equations.

Before doing so, we must return to the governing equations themselves.

### 1.2 Governing Equations for Steady Compressible Flow

We consider steady, inviscid, compressible flow in two spatial dimensions. The starting point for our analysis is the system of conservation laws derived in earlier modules. In primitive form, these equations may be written as

$$
\begin{align*}
\nabla \cdot(\rho \boldsymbol{u}) & =0  \tag{1.1}\\
\rho(\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla) \boldsymbol{u} & =-\nabla p \tag{1.2}
\end{align*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{u}=(u, v)$ is the velocity field, $\rho$ is the density, and $p$ is the pressure.
To close the system, we use a thermodynamic relation appropriate for smooth regions of the flow. In such regions, entropy is constant along streamlines, and the flow may be treated as isentropic. Under this assumption, pressure and density are related by

$$
\begin{equation*}
d p=a^{2} d \rho \tag{1.3}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $a$ is the local speed of sound.
It is useful to interpret this assumption in the broader context of compressible flows. Entropy variations in real flows are typically generated by shocks or viscous effects. However, once generated, these variations are convected with the flow. As a result, regions between such events may often be treated as isentropic, with the effects of upstream entropy changes incorporated through boundary or initial conditions. In this sense, the isentropic assumption reflects a focus on the local structure of smooth flow regions rather than a restriction to globally isentropic flows.

A similar but distinct consideration applies to vorticity. While vorticity may be generated by viscous effects, curved shocks, or baroclinic mechanisms, it is not dissipated in inviscid flow. Once present, it persists and influences the downstream evolution of the flow. The present formulation therefore allows for both irrotational and rotational states, provided that the rotationality was introduced by upstream mechanisms (such as viscous effects or shocks) prior to the inviscid region being modeled. In this setting, we allow for inviscid rotational flows whose velocity field need not necessarily be expressible as a velocity potential.

Using the relation (1.3), the governing equations may be written entirely in terms of ( $u, v, \rho$ ). Expanding (1.1)-(1.2) in Cartesian coordinates and eliminating pressure derivatives, we obtain

$$
\begin{gather*}
u \rho_{x}+v \rho_{y}+\rho\left(u_{x}+v_{y}\right)=0  \tag{1.4}\\
u u_{x}+v u_{y}+\frac{a^{2}}{\rho} \rho_{x}=0  \tag{1.5}\\
u v_{x}+v v_{y}+\frac{a^{2}}{\rho} \rho_{y}=0 \tag{1.6}
\end{gather*}
$$

Equations (1.4)-(1.6) form a closed first-order system governing steady compressible flow in smooth regions. In the following sections, we will analyze the structure of this system and determine how it governs the propagation of information through the flow.

### 1.3 First-Order Structure and Characteristic Directions

In the previous section, we expressed the governing equations for steady compressible flow as a system of first-order partial differential equations in the variables ( $u, v, \rho$ ). We now seek to understand the structure of this system and, in particular, whether it admits preferred directions along which the solution simplifies.

To begin, we write the system (1.4)-(1.6) in the form

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{q}) \boldsymbol{q}_{x}+\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{q}) \boldsymbol{q}_{y}=0 \tag{1.7}
\end{equation*}
$$

where

$$
\boldsymbol{q}=\left[\begin{array}{l}
u \\
v \\
\rho
\end{array}\right],
$$

and the matrices $\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{q})$ and $\boldsymbol{B}(\boldsymbol{q})$ are given by

$$
\boldsymbol{A}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
\rho & 0 & u  \tag{1.8}\\
u & 0 & \frac{a^{2}}{\rho} \\
0 & u & 0
\end{array}\right], \quad \boldsymbol{B}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
0 & \rho & v \\
v & 0 & 0 \\
0 & v & \frac{a^{2}}{\rho}
\end{array}\right] .
$$

Our goal is to determine how the structure of (1.7) governs the behavior of its solutions.

### 1.3.1 Connection with Second-Order PDE Classification

In Module 3, we developed a framework for classifying second-order partial differential equations by examining their principal part. In that setting, the highest-order derivatives could be written in the form

$$
\begin{equation*}
A \phi_{x x}+2 B \phi_{x y}+C \phi_{y y} \tag{1.9}
\end{equation*}
$$

which was associated with the matrix

$$
\boldsymbol{K}=\left[\begin{array}{ll}
A & B  \tag{1.10}\\
B & C
\end{array}\right]
$$

By diagonalizing $\boldsymbol{K}$, we identified canonical forms of the governing equation. In the hyperbolic case, this diagonalization led to a factorization of the differential operator into directional derivatives, revealing characteristic curves along which the equation simplifies.

We now ask the analogous question for the first-order system (1.7): is there a representation in which the system decouples into simpler equations along preferred directions in the $(x, y)$ plane?

### 1.3.2 Diagonalization and Characteristic Directions

To answer this question, we consider the behavior of the system locally at a given state $\boldsymbol{q}_{*}$. Treating the coefficients as locally constant, we may rewrite (1.7) as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{q}_{x}+\boldsymbol{C}_{*} \boldsymbol{q}_{y}=0 \tag{1.11}
\end{equation*}
$$

where

$$
\begin{equation*}
C_{*}=\boldsymbol{A}\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{*}\right)^{-1} \boldsymbol{B}\left(\boldsymbol{q}_{*}\right) . \tag{1.12}
\end{equation*}
$$

We now diagonalize the matrix $C_{\text {* }}$ :

$$
\begin{equation*}
C_{*}=\boldsymbol{R} \boldsymbol{\Lambda} \boldsymbol{R}^{-1} \tag{1.13}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\lambda_{1}, \lambda_{2}, \lambda_{3}\right)$ contains the eigenvalues of $\boldsymbol{C}_{*}$.
Defining a new set of variables

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{R}^{-1} \boldsymbol{q} \tag{1.14}
\end{equation*}
$$

we obtain the decoupled system

$$
\begin{equation*}
w_{i, x}+\lambda_{i} w_{i, y}=0, \quad i=1,2,3 \tag{1.15}
\end{equation*}
$$

Each equation in (1.15) has a simple interpretation. Consider a curve in the $(x, y)$ plane defined by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d y}{d x}=\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{q}) \tag{1.16}
\end{equation*}
$$

Along such a curve, the total derivative of $w_{i}$ is given by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d w_{i}}{d x}=w_{i, x}+\frac{d y}{d x} w_{i, y} \tag{1.17}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting (1.16) into (1.15), we find

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d w_{i}}{d x}=0 \tag{1.18}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, along curves satisfying $\frac{d y}{d x}=\lambda_{i}(\boldsymbol{q})$, the quantity $w_{i}$ remains constant. These curves are the characteristic curves of the system.

### 1.3.3 Classification and Interpretation

The nature of the eigenvalues $\lambda_{i}$ determines the type of the system. If all eigenvalues are real and the matrix $\boldsymbol{C}_{*}$ admits a complete set of eigenvectors, then there exist real curves in the physical $(x, y)$ plane along which information propagates. In this case, the system is said to be hyperbolic.

If the eigenvalues are complex, no such real directions exist, and the system is elliptic. Repeated eigenvalues with incomplete eigenvectors correspond to a parabolic system.

This classification parallels that of second-order equations. In both settings, hyperbolicity is associated with the existence of real directions along which the governing equations simplify and along which information travels. The key difference is that, for first-order systems, this structure emerges directly through diagonalization of the coefficient matrices, rather than through factorization of a second-order operator.

### 1.4 Characteristic Directions in Compressible Flow

In the previous section, we established that the structure of the governing equations is determined by the eigenvalues of the matrix $\boldsymbol{A}^{-1} \boldsymbol{B}$. These eigenvalues define characteristic directions in the $(x, y)$ plane along which the governing equations simplify. We now compute these directions explicitly for the system (1.7) and interpret their physical significance.

### 1.4.1 Computation of Characteristic Directions

The characteristic directions are determined by the condition

$$
\begin{equation*}
\operatorname{det}(\boldsymbol{B}-\lambda \boldsymbol{A})=0 \tag{1.19}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $\lambda=d y / d x$.
Evaluating (1.19) using the matrices (1.8) gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
(v-\lambda u)\left[(v-\lambda u)^{2}-a^{2}\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right)\right]=0 \tag{1.20}
\end{equation*}
$$

The linear factor yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
\lambda=\frac{v}{u}=\tan \theta \tag{1.21}
\end{equation*}
$$

which corresponds to the streamline direction.
The quadratic factor yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
(v-\lambda u)^{2}=a^{2}\left(1+\lambda^{2}\right) \tag{1.22}
\end{equation*}
$$

To develop a closed-form expression for the second and third characteristic directions, it is convenient to express the velocity in terms of its magnitude and direction:

$$
\begin{equation*}
u=V \cos \theta, \quad v=V \sin \theta \tag{1.23}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $V=\sqrt{u^{2}+v^{2}}$ is the speed and $\theta$ is the flow angle measured from the $x$-axis.
Substituting (1.23) into (1.22) and simplifying yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
\tan (\theta-\arctan \lambda)= \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}} \tag{1.24}
\end{equation*}
$$

where $M=V / a$ is the local Mach number. Using $\sin \mu=1 / M$ and $\sin ^{2} \mu+\cos ^{2} \mu=1$, we obtain

$$
\cos \mu=\frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{M}
$$

so that

$$
\tan \mu=\frac{\sin \mu}{\cos \mu}=\frac{1 / M}{\sqrt{M^{2}-1} / M}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}
$$

We may therefore rewrite the characteristic condition as

$$
\tan (\theta-\arctan \lambda)= \pm \tan \mu
$$

Thus,

$$
\begin{equation*}
\lambda=\tan (\theta \mp \mu) \tag{1.25}
\end{equation*}
$$

The assignment of the labels "+" and "-" is a matter of convention.
In these notes, we adopt the following convention. We define characteristic curves $C_{ \pm}$as

$$
C_{+}: \frac{d y}{d x}=\tan (\theta-\mu), \quad C_{-}: \frac{d y}{d x}=\tan (\theta+\mu)
$$

Arc length along a curve in each family will be denoted by $\ell_{+}$and $\ell_{-}$, respectively. This choice is made so that the $C_{+}$characteristic corresponds to the right-running (downstream-oriented) family in the characteristic diagrams used throughout the course. All subsequent results will be interpreted consistently with this convention.

The three characteristic directions correspond to the three eigenvalues of the system. Specifically,

$$
\lambda_{1}=\tan \theta
$$

represents the streamline direction, while the remaining two eigenvalues correspond to the Machline directions:

$$
\lambda_{2}=\tan (\theta-\mu), \quad \lambda_{3}=\tan (\theta+\mu)
$$

Using the labeling convention introduced above, we identify

$$
C_{+}: \lambda_{2}=\tan (\theta-\mu), \quad C_{-}: \lambda_{3}=\tan (\theta+\mu)
$$

### 1.4.2 Interpretation

The characteristic directions depend on the local flow state through $\theta$ and $M$. Unlike the linear small-disturbance setting of Module 4, these directions are not fixed, but vary throughout the flow.

Real characteristic directions exist only when $M>1$. In this case, $\mu$ is real and the system is hyperbolic. When $M<1$, the Mach angle becomes imaginary, and no real characteristic directions exist. This corresponds to elliptic behavior.

The two families of characteristic curves define directions along which information propagates through the flow. The solution at a given point depends only on data carried along these curves from upstream regions.

### 1.4.3 Preview of the Solution Structure

The identification of characteristic directions provides the backdrop for which solutions can be constructed. At a high level, the solution process is as follows:

1. At a given point, the local state determines the characteristic directions through (1.25).
2. Along each characteristic curve, the governing equations reduce to ordinary differential relations for appropriately defined variables. We showed in (1.18) that an ODE exists along special directions, though in a later chapter we will use a form more conducive to computation.
3. Known data from upstream or boundary conditions may be propagated along these curves to determine the flow at new points.
4. Since the characteristic directions depend on the solution, they must be updated as the flow evolves.

Thus, unlike the linear case studied previously, the geometry of the characteristic curves and the evolution of the solution are coupled. The flow field is constructed by simultaneously determining both.

In the next chapter, we will develop the equations governing the evolution of the flow variables along these characteristic curves.

## Chapter 2

## Governing ODEs Along Characteristics

### 2.1 Specialization to Irrotational Flow and Flow-Aligned Coordinates

In Chapter 1, we showed that the governing equations of steady compressible flow admit characteristic directions determined by the eigenvalues of the system (1.7). For planar flow, these directions are given by (1.25). These directions are defined relative to the flow itself, suggesting that both the governing equations and their solutions are most naturally expressed in a coordinate system aligned with the local flow direction.

Our goal in this chapter is to determine how the flow evolves along these characteristic curves.

## Reduction of the governing equations

The evolution along characteristic curves may be obtained directly from the full system (1.4)(1.6), which governs steady, inviscid, isentropic flow in the variables ( $u, v, \rho$ ). That formulation accommodates rotational flow and retains all thermodynamic variables.

For many smooth compressible flows of interest, however, it is appropriate to specialize to irrotational flow. In this setting, the governing equations simplify in a fundamental way.

Recall that the steady inviscid momentum equation may be written as

$$
\begin{equation*}
(\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla) \boldsymbol{u}+\frac{1}{\rho} \nabla p=0 \tag{2.1}
\end{equation*}
$$

Using the identity

$$
\begin{equation*}
(\boldsymbol{u} \cdot \nabla) \boldsymbol{u}=\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\right)-\boldsymbol{u} \times(\nabla \times \boldsymbol{u}) \tag{2.2}
\end{equation*}
$$

we see that, for irrotational flow,

$$
\begin{equation*}
\nabla \times \boldsymbol{u}=0 \tag{2.3}
\end{equation*}
$$

the momentum equation reduces to

$$
\begin{equation*}
\nabla\left(\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho} \nabla p=0 \tag{2.4}
\end{equation*}
$$

For isentropic flow, $\frac{1}{\rho} \nabla p=\nabla h$, so that

$$
\begin{equation*}
\nabla\left(h+\frac{1}{2}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\right)=0 \tag{2.5}
\end{equation*}
$$

and therefore

$$
\begin{equation*}
h+\frac{1}{2}\left(u^{2}+v^{2}\right)=h_{0} \tag{2.6}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, in steady, inviscid, irrotational flow, the governing equations reduce to continuity and irrotationality, with Bernoulli's equation providing a relation between velocity magnitude and thermodynamic state. The system is therefore reduced from three variables to two.

This reduction is reflected in the characteristic structure. In the full $3 \times 3$ system, there are three characteristic directions: the streamline direction $\lambda=\tan \theta$ and the two Mach-line directions $\lambda=\tan (\theta \pm \mu)$. In the reduced formulation, only two independent characteristic families remain.

The streamline direction $\lambda=\tan \theta$ corresponds to transport along particle paths. In the full system, this direction carries independent information about the evolution of thermodynamic variables. In the reduced system, however, Bernoulli's equation already enforces this relation along streamlines. As a result, the streamline direction does not produce an independent compatibility relation and drops out of the characteristic structure.

The remaining characteristic directions,

$$
C_{+}: \lambda=\tan (\theta-\mu), \quad C_{-}: \lambda=\tan (\theta+\mu)
$$

are the directions along which independent compatibility relations persist.

## Formulation in terms of $V$ and $\theta$

We now express the governing equations in terms of the velocity magnitude and flow angle, defined in Chapter 1. Differentiating Bernoulli's equation (2.6) and using the thermodynamic relations introduced in Chapter 1 gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\rho_{x}=-\frac{\rho V}{a^{2}} V_{x}, \quad \rho_{y}=-\frac{\rho V}{a^{2}} V_{y} \tag{2.7}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting (2.7) into the continuity equation (1.4) yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
u_{x}+v_{y}-\frac{V}{a^{2}}\left(u V_{x}+v V_{y}\right)=0 \tag{2.8}
\end{equation*}
$$

Together with the irrotationality condition

$$
\begin{equation*}
v_{x}-u_{y}=0 \tag{2.9}
\end{equation*}
$$

equations (2.8) and (2.9) form a closed system for ( $V, \theta$ ).
Substituting (1.23) into these equations and simplifying yields

$$
\begin{gather*}
\left(1-M^{2}\right)\left(\cos \theta V_{x}+\sin \theta V_{y}\right)+V\left(-\sin \theta \theta_{x}+\cos \theta \theta_{y}\right)=0  \tag{2.10}\\
\sin \theta V_{x}-\cos \theta V_{y}+V\left(\cos \theta \theta_{x}+\sin \theta \theta_{y}\right)=0 \tag{2.11}
\end{gather*}
$$

where $M=V / a$ is the local Mach number.

## Flow-aligned coordinate representation

Motivated by the characteristic directions (1.25), we introduce derivatives aligned with and normal to the flow direction:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial}{\partial s}=\cos \theta \frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\sin \theta \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \quad \frac{\partial}{\partial n}=-\sin \theta \frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\cos \theta \frac{\partial}{\partial y} . \tag{2.12}
\end{equation*}
$$

Using these definitions, (2.10)-(2.11) become

$$
\begin{gather*}
\left(1-M^{2}\right) V_{s}+V \theta_{n}=0,  \tag{2.13}\\
V_{n}-V \theta_{s}=0 .  \tag{2.14}\\
\left(1-M^{2}\right) V_{s}+V \theta_{n}=0, \quad V_{n}-V \theta_{s}=0 . \tag{2.15}
\end{gather*}
$$

These equations express the coupling between flow acceleration and turning in directions aligned with the local velocity. They will serve as the starting point for deriving the governing ordinary differential equations along characteristic curves.

## Axisymmetric analogue

For steady, inviscid, irrotational, axisymmetric flow (without swirl), the same procedure yields

$$
\begin{gather*}
\left(1-M^{2}\right) V_{s}+V \theta_{n}+\frac{V \sin \theta}{y}=0  \tag{2.16}\\
V_{n}-V \theta_{s}=0 \tag{2.17}
\end{gather*}
$$

where $y$ is the radial coordinate. The additional term reflects the geometric divergence of streamlines.

With the governing equations now expressed in flow-aligned form, we are ready to combine them with the characteristic directions (1.25) to derive ordinary differential equations along those curves.

### 2.2 Governing ODEs Along Characteristic Curves

Our goal is now to express the governing equations (2.15) as an ODE by casting them on the characteristic curves defined by (1.25). But which two of the three possible characteristics should we look along?

Let us examine the structure of the governing equations (2.15). These equations are already written along the streamline coordinate, and the governing equations do not reduce to an ODE. As such, we can deduce that the curve defined by $\tan (\theta)$ is the characteristic direction that "drops out" in moving from our rotational to our irrotational formulation. We will therefore proceed in what follows, looking to cast the governing equations (2.15) along the special characteristic curves $C_{ \pm}$.

## Geometric relation between ( $s, n$ ) and characteristic directions

At a given point in the flow, the unit vector tangent to the flow direction is

$$
\boldsymbol{e}_{s}=(\cos \theta, \sin \theta)
$$

while the unit normal is

$$
\boldsymbol{e}_{n}=(-\sin \theta, \cos \theta)
$$

Recall our focus on the characteristic curves defined by $C_{ \pm}: \frac{d y}{d x}=\tan (\theta \mp \mu)$. It is therefore natural to associate to each characteristic family a unit tangent vector

$$
\begin{equation*}
e_{\ell_{ \pm}}=(\cos (\theta \mp \mu), \sin (\theta \mp \mu)) \tag{2.18}
\end{equation*}
$$

We parametrize each characteristic curve by its arc length $\ell_{ \pm}$, so that $\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{ \pm}\left(\ell_{ \pm}\right)$. By construction, $\frac{d x_{ \pm}}{d \ell_{ \pm}}$is the unit tangent vector to the curve. Since the characteristic curves are tangent to directions making angle $\theta \mp \mu$ with the $x$-axis, it follows that

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d x_{ \pm}}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=e_{\ell_{ \pm}} \tag{2.19}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, $\ell_{ \pm}$measures distance along the characteristic curves, and derivatives with respect to $\ell_{ \pm}$ represent rates of change along those curves.

Using standard trigonometric identities, this direction may be expressed in terms of the flowaligned basis as

$$
\begin{equation*}
\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell_{ \pm}}=\cos \mu \boldsymbol{e}_{s} \mp \sin \mu \boldsymbol{e}_{n} \tag{2.20}
\end{equation*}
$$

This relation provides the geometric link between the $(s, n)$ coordinate system and the characteristic directions: each characteristic direction is obtained by rotating the flow direction by an angle $\pm \mu$.

## Derivatives along characteristic curves

Let $\ell_{ \pm}$denote the arc-length parameter along the characteristic curves corresponding to the directions $\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell_{ \pm}}$. Thus, each characteristic curve may be written in parametric form as $\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{ \pm}\left(\ell_{ \pm}\right)$.

For any scalar field $f(\boldsymbol{x})$, the derivative along the characteristic is defined by

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d f}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=\nabla f \cdot \frac{d \boldsymbol{x}_{ \pm}}{d \ell_{ \pm}} \tag{2.21}
\end{equation*}
$$

That is,

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=e_{\ell_{ \pm}} \cdot \nabla \tag{2.22}
\end{equation*}
$$

Using (2.20) in (2.22), we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=\cos \mu \frac{\partial}{\partial s} \mp \sin \mu \frac{\partial}{\partial n} \tag{2.23}
\end{equation*}
$$

This directional derivative represents the rate of change of a field as one moves along a characteristic curve. The expression shows that derivatives along characteristic curves are linear combinations of derivatives along and normal to the flow direction.

## Construction of the governing ODEs

We now combine the flow-aligned system (2.15) with the characteristic directions derived in Chapter 1.

Recall that derivatives along the characteristic curves are given by (2.23), so that

$$
\begin{equation*}
V_{\ell_{ \pm}}=\cos \mu V_{s} \mp \sin \mu V_{n}, \quad \theta_{\ell_{ \pm}}=\cos \mu \theta_{s} \mp \sin \mu \theta_{n} \tag{2.24}
\end{equation*}
$$

From (2.15), we express the normal derivatives in terms of tangential derivatives:

$$
\begin{equation*}
V_{n}=V \theta_{s}, \quad \theta_{n}=\frac{M^{2}-1}{V} V_{s} \tag{2.25}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting (2.25) into (2.24) yields a linear system for the unknown derivatives $V_{s}$ and $\theta_{s}$ :

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
\cos \mu & \mp \sin \mu V  \tag{2.26}\\
\mp \sin \mu \frac{M^{2}-1}{V} & \cos \mu
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{l}
V_{s} \\
\theta_{s}
\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}
V_{\ell_{ \pm}} \\
\theta_{\ell_{ \pm}}
\end{array}\right] .
$$

From the definition of the Mach angle,

$$
\mu=\sin ^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{M}\right)
$$

we have

$$
\begin{equation*}
\sin \mu=\frac{1}{M}, \quad \cos \mu=\frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{M} . \tag{2.27}
\end{equation*}
$$

Using (2.27), one finds that the determinant of the coefficient matrix in (2.26) vanishes identically. This is precisely the signature that $e_{ \pm}$are characteristic directions; i.e., that $C_{ \pm}$are characteristic curves.

Because the matrix is singular, the system (2.26) is solvable only if its right-hand side lies in the range of the matrix. Equivalently, the right-hand side must be orthogonal to a left null vector of the coefficient matrix. A convenient choice is

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ll} 
\pm \frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{V} & 1
\end{array}\right],
$$

which leads directly to the compatibility relation

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta_{\ell_{ \pm}} \pm \frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{V} V_{\ell_{ \pm}}=0 \tag{2.28}
\end{equation*}
$$

Equivalently, along each characteristic curve,

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d \theta}{d \ell_{ \pm}} \pm \frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{V} \frac{d V}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=0 \tag{2.29}
\end{equation*}
$$

## Interpretation

Equation (2.28) shows that along each characteristic family, the two-dimensional system reduces to an ordinary differential relation coupling the turning of the flow and its acceleration. The two families correspond to the two directions $\theta \pm \mu$, and along each family, a specific combination of $V$ and $\theta$ remains invariant.

This is the concrete realization, in physical variables, of the characteristic structure identified abstractly in Chapter 1: along special directions in the flow, the governing PDEs reduce to ODEs.

## Axisymmetric analogue

For axisymmetric flow, the same procedure yields

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta_{\ell_{ \pm}} \pm \frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{V} V_{\ell_{ \pm}}= \pm \frac{\sin \theta}{M y} \tag{2.30}
\end{equation*}
$$

The right-hand side represents a geometric source term arising from the divergence of streamlines in axisymmetric flow. The characteristic geometry remains unchanged, but the evolution along the characteristics is modified by this additional term.

## Chapter 3

## Constructing Solutions via Characteristics

This chapter proceeds in three steps. First, we identify how the solution at a single point is determined by information carried along characteristic curves from nearby points. Second, we convert the compatibility relations derived in Chapter 2 into algebraic relations that can be evaluated at such a point. Finally, we use this local construction repeatedly to build the solution throughout a domain.

### 3.1 Local Construction of the Solution

Let $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}=\left(x_{P}, y_{P}\right)$ be a point in the flow field at which the flow variables

$$
V\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right), \quad \theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)
$$

are unknown.
Through $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ pass two characteristic curves, corresponding to the characteristic directions (1.25) derived in Chapter 1. Along each characteristic, information propagates from upstream points in the flow.

Let $\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{+}}$and $\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{-}}$denote nearby points located upstream along the $C_{ \pm}$characteristic curves passing through $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$. Since the governing equations reduce to ordinary differential relations along these curves (Chapter 2), the flow variables at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ are determined by information carried along these two characteristics.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/1fcb9af7-df15-41a5-897b-e8cec3f56db1-15.jpg?height=257&width=362&top_left_y=2105&top_left_x=880)
Figure 3.1: The solution at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ is determined by information arriving along the two characteristic families.

At $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$, we have two unknowns, $V$ and $\theta$. Each characteristic provides one relation between these quantities, and together they determine the local flow state.

Key idea: The solution is constructed point-by-point from the intersection of characteristic information.

### 3.2 Algebraic Construction at a Point

The compatibility relations derived in Chapter 2 describe how the flow variables evolve along characteristic curves:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d \theta}{d \ell_{ \pm}} \pm \frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{V} \frac{d V}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=0 \tag{3.1}
\end{equation*}
$$

Although $V$ and $\theta$ are functions of position $\boldsymbol{x}$, the compatibility relations are evaluated along characteristic curves $\boldsymbol{x}_{ \pm}\left(\ell_{ \pm}\right)$introduced in Chapter 2 . Along such a curve, the composition $V\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{ \pm}\left(\ell_{ \pm}\right)\right)$ defines a single-variable function of $\ell_{ \pm}$, and

$$
\frac{d}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=e_{\ell_{ \pm}} \cdot \nabla
$$

represents the directional derivative along the curve. The derivatives in (3.1) are therefore rates of change of the flow variables as one moves along the characteristic.

## From compatibility relations to invariants

We return to the compatibility relations (3.1). As written, this expression has a particular structure: the first term involves only $\theta$, while the second involves only $V$ (through $M=V / a$ ).

In fact, the expression closely resembles a total derivative along the characteristic curve. Specifically, if we could find a function $v(V)$ that satisfies

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d v}{d V}=\frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{V} \tag{3.2}
\end{equation*}
$$

then the compatibility relations would simplify to

$$
\frac{d}{d \ell_{ \pm}}(\theta \pm v(V))=0
$$

This result would indeed recover (3.1) since carrying the $d / d \ell_{ \pm}$through would give

$$
\frac{d \theta}{d \ell_{ \pm}} \pm \frac{d v}{d \ell_{ \pm}}=\frac{d \theta}{d \ell_{ \pm}} \pm \frac{d v}{d V} \frac{d V}{d \ell_{ \pm}}
$$

which produces (3.1) if $v$ is constructed to satisfy (3.2).
This form is particularly useful: it expresses the compatibility relations as a statement that a single quantity, $\theta \pm v(V)$, is conserved along each characteristic curve. Such invariants allow us to relate flow variables at different points along a characteristic without solving a differential equation explicitly, and will form the basis of our solution procedure.

## Evaluation of $v$

To evaluate $v$, we express (3.2) in terms of the Mach number $M=V / a$. Using the chain rule,

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d v}{d M}=\frac{d v}{d V} \frac{d V}{d M} \tag{3.3}
\end{equation*}
$$

The first factor is given by (3.2). To determine $\frac{d V}{d M}$, we use Bernoulli's equation (2.6). For a calorically perfect gas,

$$
h=\frac{a^{2}}{\gamma-1}
$$

so Bernoulli's equation becomes

$$
\frac{a^{2}}{\gamma-1}+\frac{V^{2}}{2}=\text { constant. }
$$

Substituting $V=a M$ yields

$$
a^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma-1}+\frac{M^{2}}{2}\right)=\text { constant }
$$

or equivalently,

$$
\begin{equation*}
a^{2}\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2}\right)=\text { constant }=: c_{1} \tag{3.4}
\end{equation*}
$$

That is,

$$
\begin{equation*}
a=\sqrt{\frac{c_{1}}{\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2}\right)}} . \tag{3.5}
\end{equation*}
$$

Differentiating $V=a M$ with respect to $M$, using (3.5) for $a$, (details omitted for brevity) gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d V}{d M}=\frac{V}{M\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2}\right)} \tag{3.6}
\end{equation*}
$$

Substituting (3.2) and (3.6) into (3.3), we obtain

$$
\begin{equation*}
\frac{d v}{d M}=\frac{\sqrt{M^{2}-1}}{M\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2} M^{2}\right)} \tag{3.7}
\end{equation*}
$$

Integrating (3.7) (details omitted) yields the Prandtl-Meyer function

$$
\begin{equation*}
v(M)=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+1}{\gamma-1}} \tan ^{-1}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma+1}\left(M^{2}-1\right)}\right)-\tan ^{-1}\left(\sqrt{M^{2}-1}\right) \tag{3.8}
\end{equation*}
$$

## Algebraic system at a point

We now use the invariant quantities identified above to relate the flow variables at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ to those at upstream points along each characteristic.

Recall that there are invariants preserved along characteristic curves as:

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta+v(V)=\text { constant along } C_{+}, \quad \theta-v(V)=\text { constant along } C_{-} . \tag{3.9}
\end{equation*}
$$

Consider the two characteristic curves passing through $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$. Let $\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{+}}$and $\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{-}}$be upstream points along the + and - characteristics, respectively. Since the invariants are constant along each curve, we may equate their values at these points and at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ :

$$
\begin{align*}
& \theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)+v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)\right)=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{+}}\right)+v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{+}}\right)\right)  \tag{3.10}\\
& \theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)-v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)\right)=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{-}}\right)-v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{-}}\right)\right) \tag{3.11}
\end{align*}
$$

Define these invariants carried along each characteristic as

$$
I_{+}=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{+}}\right)+v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{+}}\right)\right), \quad I_{-}=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{-}}\right)-v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P_{-}}\right)\right) .
$$

Then (3.10)-(3.11) yield a system of two algebraic equations for the unknown values at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$. For notational clarity, we now write

$$
\theta:=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right), \quad M:=M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right),
$$

so that the system becomes

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta+v(M)=I_{+}, \quad \theta-v(M)=I_{-} \tag{3.12}
\end{equation*}
$$

Solving (3.12) gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta=\frac{I_{+}+I_{-}}{2}, \quad v(M)=\frac{I_{+}-I_{-}}{2} \tag{3.13}
\end{equation*}
$$

Thus, the flow angle at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ is obtained directly, while the Mach number is determined by inverting the function $v(M)$. The velocity magnitude then follows from $V=a M$, with $a$ determined from (3.5).

### 3.3 Constructing the Full Flow Field by Characteristic Marching

## Geometry of the marching process

In Section 1, we saw that the flow state at a single point $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ may be determined from information carried along the two characteristic curves passing through that point. In Section 2, we converted this observation into an algebraic procedure: given two upstream points, one along each characteristic family, we can determine the flow variables at their intersection.

We now extend this idea to construct the solution throughout a region of the flow.
Suppose that the flow variables are known along a curve in the domain. This curve may represent, for example, a boundary or an initial line of data. Our goal is to use this known information to construct the solution at nearby points.

Consider two neighboring points $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$ along the known curve. From $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$, we trace a + characteristic into the domain, while from $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$, we trace a - characteristic. These two characteristic curves intersect at a new point, denoted $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$. As established in Section 1, the flow variables at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ are determined by the information carried along these two characteristics.

By repeating this construction for successive pairs of points along the known curve, we obtain a new set of points lying on a second curve in the domain. This new curve represents the next "row" of the solution. The process may then be repeated, using the newly constructed curve as the starting point for the next step.

![](https://cdn.mathpix.com/cropped/1fcb9af7-df15-41a5-897b-e8cec3f56db1-19.jpg?height=205&width=669&top_left_y=696&top_left_x=728)
Figure 3.2: Construction of new solution points by intersecting characteristic curves from neighboring upstream points.

This construction highlights the fundamental structure of the method. Each new point is obtained as the intersection of two characteristic curves, and the solution is advanced by repeatedly applying this local construction.

Key idea: A line of known data generates a new line of solution points through the intersection of characteristic curves.

## Determination of the intersection point

We now describe how to determine the location of the new point $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ constructed in the previous subsection.

Let $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}=\left(x_{A}, y_{A}\right)$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}=\left(x_{B}, y_{B}\right)$ be two neighboring points along a curve of known data. At each point, the flow variables $V$ and $\theta$ are known, and hence the Mach number $M$ and Mach angle $\mu$ are also known.

From $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$, the $C_{+}$characteristic is defined by the slope

$$
\frac{d y}{d x}=\tan \left(\theta_{A}-\mu_{A}\right)
$$

so that its equation may be written in point-slope form as

$$
\begin{equation*}
y-y_{A}=\tan \left(\theta_{A}-\mu_{A}\right)\left(x-x_{A}\right) . \tag{3.14}
\end{equation*}
$$

Similarly, from $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$, the $C_{-}$characteristic satisfies

$$
\frac{d y}{d x}=\tan \left(\theta_{B}+\mu_{B}\right)
$$

and its equation is

$$
\begin{equation*}
y-y_{B}=\tan \left(\theta_{B}+\mu_{B}\right)\left(x-x_{B}\right) \tag{3.15}
\end{equation*}
$$

The intersection point $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}=\left(x_{P}, y_{P}\right)$ is obtained by solving (3.14)-(3.15) simultaneously. This yields a $2 \times 2$ linear system for $x_{P}$ and $y_{P}$. Eliminating $y$ gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
x_{P}=\frac{y_{B}-y_{A}+\tan \left(\theta_{A}-\mu_{A}\right) x_{A}-\tan \left(\theta_{B}+\mu_{B}\right) x_{B}}{\tan \left(\theta_{A}-\mu_{A}\right)-\tan \left(\theta_{B}+\mu_{B}\right)}, \tag{3.16}
\end{equation*}
$$

and $y_{P}$ follows by substitution into either (3.14) or (3.15).
This construction uses only the known values at $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$. In particular, the slopes of the characteristic curves are evaluated using the upstream flow state, and the curves are treated as straight lines between these points and $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$. This corresponds to a local linearization of the characteristic geometry.

Remark. For the construction to remain accurate, the points $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$ should be sufficiently close so that the characteristic directions do not vary significantly between them. In practice, this leads to a structured discretization in which neighboring points are chosen so that the resulting intersections lie within a controlled region of the flow.

## Determination of the flow variables at the new point

With the location of the point $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ determined, we now compute the corresponding flow variables. This step is independent of the geometric construction of $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ and relies solely on the invariant relations derived earlier.

Let $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$ and $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$ be the upstream points used to construct $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$, as described in the previous subsection. The invariant quantities along the $C_{+}$and $C_{-}$characteristic families are given by (3.9). Evaluating these invariants at $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}, \boldsymbol{x}_{B}$, and $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ yields

$$
\begin{align*}
& \theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)+v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)\right)=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{A}\right)+v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{A}\right)\right),  \tag{3.17}\\
& \theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)-v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right)\right)=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{B}\right)-v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{B}\right)\right) . \tag{3.18}
\end{align*}
$$

Define the constants carried along each characteristic as

$$
I_{+}=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{A}\right)+v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{A}\right)\right), \quad I_{-}=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{B}\right)-v\left(M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{B}\right)\right) .
$$

For notational clarity, we now denote

$$
\theta:=\theta\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right), \quad M:=M\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{P}\right),
$$

so that (3.17)-(3.18) become the algebraic system

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta+v(M)=I_{+}, \quad \theta-v(M)=I_{-} \tag{3.19}
\end{equation*}
$$

Solving (3.19) gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta=\frac{I_{+}+I_{-}}{2}, \quad v(M)=\frac{I_{+}-I_{-}}{2} \tag{3.20}
\end{equation*}
$$

The Mach number at $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ is then obtained by inverting the function $v(M)$. Since $v(M)$ does not admit a simple closed-form inverse, this step is typically carried out numerically, for example using a root-finding method or a precomputed lookup table.

Finally, the velocity magnitude is recovered from

$$
V=a M,
$$

with the sound speed $a$ determined from (3.5). The constant $c_{1}$ in that relation is fixed by the upstream flow state through Bernoulli's equation, and is therefore known from the initial data. Equivalently, $a$ may be computed directly as a function of $M$ using this relation.

## Marching algorithm

We now combine the geometric construction of Section 3.1 with the algebraic relations of Section 3.3 to obtain a procedure for constructing the flow field throughout a domain.

Starting from a curve along which the flow variables are known, the solution is advanced by repeatedly constructing new points through the intersection of characteristic curves and evaluating the corresponding flow variables.

```
Algorithm 1 Characteristic marching procedure
    Given: initial curve with known values of $V, \theta$, and $M$
    for each pair of neighboring points $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}, \boldsymbol{x}_{B}$ along the current curve do
        Compute $\mu_{A}, \mu_{B}$ from $M_{A}, M_{B}$
        Construct $C_{+}$characteristic from $\boldsymbol{x}_{A}$ and $C_{-}$characteristic from $\boldsymbol{x}_{B}$ (see (3.14)-(3.15))
        Determine intersection point $\boldsymbol{x}_{P}$ (see (3.16))
        Compute invariants
            $I_{+}=\theta_{A}+v\left(M_{A}\right), \quad I_{-}=\theta_{B}-v\left(M_{B}\right)$
        Solve
            $\theta_{P}=\frac{I_{+}+I_{-}}{2}, \quad v\left(M_{P}\right)=\frac{I_{+}-I_{-}}{2}$
        Determine $M_{P}$ by inverting $v(M)$
        Compute $V_{P}=a\left(M_{P}\right) M_{P}$ (see (3.5))
        Store ( $\boldsymbol{x}_{p}, V_{p}, \theta_{p}, M_{p}$ )
    end for
    Use the newly constructed points as the next curve and repeat
```

This procedure advances the solution by one layer of points. Repeated application constructs the flow field throughout the domain.

Key idea: Each new layer of the solution is generated by intersecting characteristics from the previous layer and applying the invariant relations.

Point redistribution along a layer. The characteristic marching procedure determines how information propagates through the domain, but it does not by itself guarantee that the computational points remain well distributed. As the solution evolves, neighboring points along a newly constructed layer may become unevenly spaced, leading to clustering or excessive separation.

To maintain accuracy and stability, it is often desirable to redistribute the points along each layer. A simple approach is as follows. After constructing a new layer of points $\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\right\}$, we define a
one-dimensional coordinate along the layer, for example the cumulative arc length

$$
s_{0}=0, \quad s_{i}=s_{i-1}+\left\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i-1}\right\| .
$$

We then introduce a new set of points $\left\{\tilde{s}_{j}\right\}$ that are uniformly spaced in $s$, and interpolate the flow variables $(\theta, M)$ (or equivalently $(\theta, v)$ ) from the original points onto these new locations. The corresponding physical coordinates $\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}$ are obtained by interpolating the geometry of the layer in the same manner.

This redistribution step produces a new curve of points with controlled spacing, which may then be used as the starting curve for the next marching step. It is important to note that this procedure does not alter the characteristic relations themselves; it is a numerical device used to maintain a well-resolved representation of the solution.

## Remarks and extensions

The characteristic marching procedure developed in this chapter provides a direct and physically meaningful way to construct solutions of the governing equations. Its structure reflects the underlying hyperbolic nature of the equations: information propagates along characteristic curves, and the solution at any point is determined by data carried along these directions.

Geometric interpretation. Each point in the flow is influenced by two upstream directions, corresponding to the two characteristic families. The solution is therefore built as the intersection of information carried along these directions. This stands in contrast to elliptic problems, where the solution at a point depends on information from the entire domain.

Accuracy considerations. The construction of new points assumes that characteristic directions remain approximately constant between neighboring points. This introduces a local approximation whose accuracy depends on the spacing of the points. In regions where the flow varies rapidly, finer spacing is required to maintain accuracy.

Extension to axisymmetric flow. The geometric construction of characteristic curves remains unchanged in the axisymmetric case. However, the compatibility relations acquire additional source terms (see Chapter 2), which modify the algebraic step used to determine the flow variables at each point. The overall marching procedure remains the same.

Limitations. The method developed here applies to smooth, isentropic regions of the flow. In the presence of shocks or other discontinuities, additional relations are required to determine the flow across these features. Such situations will be addressed separately.

## Centered Expansion Fans and the Prandtl-Meyer Relation

### 4.1 Physical setup: centered expansion from a convex corner

We now return to a problem that you will probably have seen in an undergraduate compressible flow course: the turning of a supersonic flow around a sharp convex corner. In that setting, one often learns the Prandtl-Meyer relation as a useful formula connecting the turning angle to the downstream Mach number.

Why revisit that problem here, after developing the full machinery of characteristics?
The reason is that the centered expansion fan generated by a sharp convex corner is a particularly elegant special case of the general method developed in the previous chapters. It is simple enough to admit a closed-form relation, but rich enough to reveal the geometric and analytic structure underlying that relation. By revisiting this familiar problem through the language of characteristics, we can see more clearly why the Prandtl-Meyer function appears, what information is carried along the characteristic curves, and how this same framework extends to more complicated smooth-turning flows.

This perspective is especially valuable because the centered fan is not the most general turning problem. If the boundary turns the flow continuously rather than through a sharp corner, the flow still adjusts through characteristic propagation, but the elegant closed-form simplifications of the centered fan are lost. In that more general setting, the machinery developed in Chapters 1-3 becomes essential. Thus, the centered expansion fan serves both as a physically important example in its own right and as a bridge to more complex applications.

We therefore consider a steady, inviscid, isentropic, and irrotational supersonic flow encountering a sharp convex corner in a solid boundary. The upstream flow is uniform, with Mach number $M_{1}$ and flow angle $\theta_{1}$. As the wall turns away from the flow, the fluid must also turn away from itself. This adjustment cannot occur through a compression shock. Instead, it takes place through a continuous expansion process.

The corner generates a family of characteristic curves that spread into the flow from a single point. Within the fan, the flow variables vary continuously from the upstream state ( $M_{1}, \theta_{1}$ ) to a downstream state $\left(M_{2}, \theta_{2}\right)$. The fact that all of these characteristics originate at the corner is what

### 4.2 Characteristic structure of the expansion fan

We now revisit the expansion fan using the characteristic framework developed in Chapters $1-3$. Our goal is to understand how the two characteristic families behave in this flow and how their structure simplifies the general marching procedure.

## Characteristic families in the fan

At every point in the flow, there are two characteristic curves defined by

$$
C_{ \pm}: \frac{d y}{d x}=\tan (\theta \mp \mu) .
$$

These curves are defined locally and remain valid throughout the flow, both inside and outside the expansion fan.

Outside the fan, the flow is uniform, so $\theta$ and $M$ are constant. In this case, both characteristic families consist of straight, parallel lines.

Within the fan, however, the flow varies continuously, and this variation affects the behavior of the characteristics in different ways for the two families.

## The $C_{+}$family: characteristics passing through the fan

In the upstream uniform region, the $C_{+}$characteristics are straight lines with slope $\tan \left(\theta_{1}-\mu_{1}\right)$. As they enter the fan, the flow variables change, and the characteristic direction evolves accordingly. The curves therefore bend smoothly as they pass through the fan, intersecting each of the $C_{-}$rays exactly once.

As the $C_{+}$characteristics exit the fan into the downstream uniform region, the flow again becomes constant, and the characteristics straighten, now with slope $\tan \left(\theta_{2}-\mu_{2}\right)$. Thus, each $C_{+}$ characteristic is straight upstream, curved within the fan, and straight again downstream.

Here is the key observation: since the $C_{+}$characteristics all originate upstream of the expansion fan, in that upstream region they must all have the same invariant. That is, the invariant $I_{+}$is not just constant along its respective $C_{+}$curve; it is the same invariant for all $C_{+}$characteristic curves. This constancy of all $I_{+}$invariants is a source of degeneracy in the problem: everything is determined by how information changes along the $C_{-}$curves, discussed next.

## The C_ family: rays from the corner

The expansion fan is formed by the $C_{-}$family of characteristics. These characteristics appear as straight rays emanating from the corner where the wall turns. The rays fill the fan region and are bounded by two distinguished members of the $C_{-}$family: the first ray, tangent to the upstream state, with direction $\theta_{1}+\mu_{1}$, and the last ray, tangent to the downstream state, with direction $\theta_{2}+\mu_{2}$.

It is tempting to think that since the C - curves associated with the expansion fan all pass through the same corner, they must have all share the same invariant $I_{-}$. But this conclusion is not correct. In fact, this point is a degenerate one. We can see this degeneracy by recognizing that the point belongs both to the upstream region with properties $\theta_{1}, M_{1}$ and the downstream region with properties $\theta_{2}, M_{2}$. And since that is true, then it must also belong to a intermediate regions with properties that continuously vary from $\theta_{1}, M_{1}$ to $\theta_{2}, M_{2}$. Thus, the characteristics that pass through this point have invariants that are constant along any one characteristic (that is the definition of an invariant), but which vary from one characteristic to the next. Since the invariants along the $C_{+}$ curves are constant across all $C_{+}$curves, it is therefore this family of $C_{-}$curves within the fan that determines the structure of the solution.

## Degeneracy of the marching construction

We now interpret the method of characteristics of Chapter 3 in the present setting. In the general case, the flow at a point is determined by the intersection of a $\mathrm{C}^{+}$and a $\mathrm{C}^{-}$characteristic. The compatibility relations along these curves,

$$
\theta+v(M)=\text { constant along } C_{+}, \quad \theta-v(M)=\text { constant along } C_{-}
$$

provide two independent relations that must be combined to determine the local flow state.
In the centered expansion fan, however, the geometry of the $C_{+}$family introduces a fundamental simplification. All $C_{+}$characteristics originate from the upstream flow region (before the fan). As a result, the invariant

$$
\theta+v(M)
$$

takes the same value along every $C^{+}$characteristic. Denoting this value by $I$, we obtain a global relation

$$
\theta+v(M)=I
$$

which holds throughout the entire fan region.
This relation may be used to express the flow angle directly in terms of the Mach number:

$$
\theta=-v(M)+I
$$

Thus, once the constant $I$ is fixed by the upstream state, the flow variables are no longer independent; they are constrained to lie on a one-parameter family of states.

The role of the $C_{-}$characteristics is now correspondingly reduced. Along each $C_{-}$characteristic, the second invariant

$$
\theta-v(M)=\mathrm{constant}
$$

still holds. However, since $\theta$ is already expressed in terms of $M$ through the global relation above, this condition does not introduce a new independent degree of freedom. Instead, it provides a relation that determines how the Mach number varies from one $C_{-}$ray to another.

In this way, the usual marching construction of Chapter 3 collapses. Rather than determining the solution at a point by solving a two-equation system arising from intersecting characteristics, the flow is determined by a single functional relation across the family of rays emanating from the corner. The $C_{+}$family fixes one invariant globally, while the $C_{-}$family governs the variation of the solution across the fan.

This is not a breakdown of the method of characteristics, but rather a special case in which the geometry of the flow reduces the two-parameter structure of the general solution to a oneparameter family. The compatibility relations derived in Chapter 2 remain fully valid; they simply take on a simpler form in this configuration.

In the next section, we use this reduced structure to determine the variation of $M$ and $\theta$ across the fan, leading directly to the Prandtl-Meyer relation.

### 4.3 Determination of the Expansion Fan

We now determine how the flow variables vary across the expansion fan. The analysis follows directly from the structure identified in the previous section.

## Global constraint from the $C_{+}$family

From the preceding discussion, the invariant associated with the $C_{+}$family,

$$
\theta+v(M)=I
$$

takes the same value along every ray of the fan. This constant is fixed by the upstream state:

$$
I=\theta_{1}+v\left(M_{1}\right)
$$

Thus, throughout the fan, the flow variables satisfy the relation

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta+v(M)=\theta_{1}+v\left(M_{1}\right) \tag{4.1}
\end{equation*}
$$

or equivalently,

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta=-v(M)+\theta_{1}+v\left(M_{1}\right) . \tag{4.2}
\end{equation*}
$$

This expression shows that the flow angle is determined entirely by the Mach number, and the solution reduces to determining how $M$ varies across the fan.

## Relation between upstream and downstream states

At the downstream edge of the fan, the flow reaches a uniform state ( $M_{2}, \theta_{2}$ ). Since ( 4.2 ) holds throughout the fan, it must also hold at this state:

$$
\theta_{2}=-v\left(M_{2}\right)+\theta_{1}+v\left(M_{1}\right)
$$

Rearranging gives

$$
\begin{equation*}
\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}=-\left(v\left(M_{2}\right)-v\left(M_{1}\right)\right) \tag{4.3}
\end{equation*}
$$

In the present geometry, the flow turns clockwise, so $\theta_{2}<\theta_{1}$. It is therefore common to introduce the positive turning magnitude

$$
\delta:=\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}>0
$$

In terms of $\delta$, (4.3) becomes the classical Prandtl-Meyer relation

$$
\begin{equation*}
\delta=v\left(M_{2}\right)-v\left(M_{1}\right) . \tag{4.4}
\end{equation*}
$$

Equation (4.4) is typically used to determine the downstream Mach number $M_{2}$ for a prescribed turning angle. In most applications, the upstream state ( $M_{1}, \theta_{1}$ ) and the turning angle $\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}$ are given, and the relation is solved for $M_{2}$.

Since the Prandtl-Meyer function $v(M)$ is nonlinear, this requires the solution of the equation

$$
v\left(M_{2}\right)=v\left(M_{1}\right)-\left(\theta_{2}-\theta_{1}\right)
$$

which is generally performed using a root-finding method (e.g., bisection or Newton iteration).

## Interpretation

Equation (4.4) provides a direct link between the turning of the flow and its acceleration. As the flow expands around the corner, the Mach number increases, and the flow angle adjusts according to the function $v(M)$.

Within the fan, each $C_{-}$ray corresponds to a distinct value of $M$, and therefore to a distinct value of $\theta$ through (4.2). In practice, these values are obtained by evaluating $v(M)$ and inverting the relation as needed.

## Connection to the characteristic structure

The simplicity of (4.4) is a direct consequence of the degeneracy discussed in the previous section. Because the $C_{+}$family originates from a uniform flow region upstream of the expansion fan, one of the two characteristic invariants is fixed globally, and the solution reduces to a one-parameter family.

The remaining invariant, associated with the $C_{-}$characteristics, governs how the flow varies across the fan. In the present configuration, this reduces to the relation (4.2), which encodes the full solution.

Thus, the Prandtl-Meyer function arises naturally as the quantity that relates turning and acceleration in a flow governed by the compatibility relations of Chapter 2.

Summary. The structure of the expansion fan reduces the general two-parameter characteristic solution to a one-parameter family of states. The resulting relation between flow angle and Mach number is given by the Prandtl-Meyer function, which determines both the internal structure of the fan and the connection between upstream and downstream states.

### 4.4 Constructing the Flow Field Within the Fan

We now describe how the full flow field within the expansion fan may be constructed. The key observation is that each ray of the fan corresponds to a distinct value of the flow angle $\theta$, which varies continuously from $\theta_{1}$ to $\theta_{2}$.
To determine the state along a given ray, we proceed as follows. We first select a value of $\theta$ within the interval $\left[\theta_{1}, \theta_{2}\right]$. The corresponding Mach number is then obtained from the global relation (4.2), which may be rearranged as

$$
\begin{equation*}
v(M)=v\left(M_{1}\right)-\left(\theta-\theta_{1}\right) \tag{4.5}
\end{equation*}
$$

This equation determines $M$ as a function of $\theta$ and is solved numerically in general.
Once $M$ is known, the Mach angle is given by

$$
\mu=\sin ^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{M}\right),
$$

and the direction of the corresponding $C_{-}$characteristic ray is

$$
\begin{equation*}
\alpha=\theta+\mu . \tag{4.6}
\end{equation*}
$$

Since each ray is a straight line emanating from the corner, its equation is

$$
y=x \tan (\alpha),
$$

and the flow variables depend only on the ray angle $\alpha$. As a result, the state ( $M, \theta$ ) is constant along each such line.

In this way, the full solution within the fan is obtained by sweeping through values of $\theta$ and computing the corresponding Mach number and ray direction. The resulting field is self-similar: the flow variables depend only on the direction relative to the corner, and not on the distance from it.

Summary. The expansion fan is constructed by selecting a value of $\theta$, solving for the corresponding Mach number using the Prandtl-Meyer relation, and assigning that state to the ray with direction $\theta+\mu$. This procedure determines the complete flow field without the need for point-by-point marching.
